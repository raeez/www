[005 h2] Executive Summary

[006 p] The global construction industry accounts for $12 trillion in annual spending, and real estate comprises $280 trillion in total value. Structural inefficiencies compound across every phase of the building lifecycle. Projects consistently run 30-50% over budget, while operational costs consume 60-80% of lifecycle expenses. Operational state, maintenance history, and compliance status remain fragmented across systems that cannot interoperate.

[007 p] Buildings are designed, constructed, and operated as static physical objects. They require constant human coordination across disconnected systems and misaligned incentives. Every operational decision, from maintenance scheduling and energy optimization to regulatory reporting, requires manual intervention across parties whose interests fundamentally conflict.

[008 p] This paper presents Smart Buildings: physical infrastructure operating as autonomous economic entities. Buildings become self-managing institutions and active assets, replacing the passive liabilities and coordination burdens of conventional construction. This requires changing how buildings operate, beyond improving sensors or software.

[009 p] The proposed institutional model is the Smart Asset.

[010 p] Smart Assets[1], as defined in Momentum's foundational framework, are autonomous economic entities that carry their complete operational history, understand their regulatory constraints, and execute their own operations without continuous human coordination. They invert the traditional paradigm from institution-centric (where banks hold assets and perform compliance) to asset-centric (where the asset itself performs compliance and navigates between institutions).

[011 p] The same structure applies to physical infrastructure. Smart Buildings embed the compliance, identity, and operational intelligence that Smart Assets embed in financial instruments. Smart Assets seek optimal regulatory treatment across jurisdictions, while Smart Buildings seek optimal operational frameworks across Special Economic Zones. Both use programmable primitives to execute operations: financial transactions for Smart Assets, and maintenance, energy optimization, and regulatory reporting for Smart Buildings.

[012 p] This requires solving six problems that current smart building initiatives ignore: shared semantic understanding, continuous provenance, collective learning, aligned incentives, transparent value attribution, and autonomous operation. Building intelligence depends on these foundations.

[013 p] Three capabilities make this possible. Broad Group's 37 years of modular manufacturing standardization provide a programmable physical substrate. Jan Bunge's computational design expertise provides the intelligence layer. Momentum's Mass Protocol infrastructure enables buildings to operate as Smart Assets across jurisdictions.

[014 p] This paper specifies how these capabilities combine in HomeOS, the first operating system for end-to-end lifecycle management of programmable buildings. It explains why the built environment will move from static structures to autonomous economic institutions.

[015 h2] Part I: The Broken Foundation

[016 h3] Chapter 1: Construction's Coordination Crisis

[017 p] The construction industry generates more economic value than any sector except finance, yet its coordination mechanisms differ from those of every other modern industry. A typical commercial project involves hundreds of architects, engineers, contractors, suppliers, operators, and regulators. They use different terminology and documentation, work under different incentives, and communicate through manual processes that assume adversarial relationships.

[018 p] Consider what happens when a mechanical engineer designs an HVAC system. The design exists in specialized software using industry-standard formats like BIM (Building Information Modeling). When construction begins, the contractor translates this design into shop drawings using different software and terminology. During installation, the actual configuration deviates from both design and shop drawings as workers adapt to field conditions. After completion, the building operator receives incomplete documentation using yet another terminology set. When the system requires maintenance years later, the service provider has no reliable record of what was actually installed or how it has been modified.

[019 p] This is standard practice. Design, as-built, and as-operated records remain disconnected, with no reliable way to reconcile them. Each interface between disciplines requires translation, and the errors introduced at each translation compound. By the time a building operates, its configuration only loosely resembles its design, and nobody has complete, accurate documentation of what exists.

[020 p] Construction projects average 30-50% cost overruns and schedule delays. Building operations consume 60-80% of total lifecycle costs, with 30% of energy wasted through suboptimal control. Reactive maintenance leaves failures to cause disruptions and emergency repairs at 3-5x the cost of scheduled work. Property transactions require months of due diligence to reconcile fragmented records.

[021 p] Incentives reinforce these failures. Developers profit by minimizing construction costs regardless of operational performance, while contractors profit from extending timelines and charging for changes. Architects and engineers bill hourly with limited accountability for building performance. Operators inherit the resulting problems and manage reactively, while insurers pay when things fail. Contracts structure the entire value chain for conflict: each party seeks maximum personal value while shifting risk and liability to others.

[022 p] Information asymmetry and blame-shifting displace optimization. Participants avoid disclosing problems early or sharing information transparently, and nobody wants to innovate when contracts discourage the coordination it requires. Despite major advances in materials, software, and construction techniques, the industry has barely improved productivity in fifty years.

[023 h3] Chapter 2: Why the PropTech Industry Has Failed

[024 p] The PropTech sector has produced dozens of "smart building" companies over the past decade, backed by billions in venture capital. Most have failed or remain marginal. Those that survived typically devolved into property management software—useful operational tools, but not a new class of infrastructure.

[025 p] The three approaches failed for different reasons.

[026 p] The first wave added sensors to otherwise passive buildings. Companies deployed IoT networks for occupancy detection, temperature monitoring, and energy measurement. Machine learning models analyzed the resulting large volumes of data to optimize operations.

[027 p] Sensors leave the underlying coordination problems intact: adversarial contracts, fragmented documentation, and misaligned incentives. They accelerate activity within those constraints. Operators may decline optimization recommendations because of other priorities. Training data may misrepresent the actual building configuration, and the metrics being optimized may conflict with stakeholder interests.

[028 p] These systems observe building operations and suggest improvements. They cannot act autonomously because they lack agency, authority, and integration with the decision-making structures that control operations. They function as dashboards rather than operating systems.

[029 p] The second wave used comprehensive building models to support intelligence. Digital twins simulate building state and allow virtual experiments before physical changes. They demonstrated that computational modeling could handle building complexity, a significant technical achievement.

[030 p] The digital twin remains separate from the physical building. Its simulation attempts to track reality but has no causal authority over it. Actual building state diverges from the model immediately and continuously, with no mechanism to reconcile the two. The technically impressive model becomes operationally irrelevant.

[031 p] Digital twins are also typically created at design time and never updated as the building evolves. They capture the architect's intent rather than the contractor's execution or the operator's modifications. Their resemblance to the actual building becomes superficial, so decisions based on them have an uncertain relationship to physical outcomes.

[032 p] Better information also leaves incentive misalignment intact. Contractors still profit from delays, and operators still manage reactively under the same adversarial contracts. Information asymmetry and blame-shifting continue despite better visualizations.

[033 p] The third wave focused on collaboration—software platforms where architects, engineers, contractors, and operators could share information and coordinate activities. These tools improved communication and reduced some coordination friction through centralized document management and task tracking.

[034 p] These platforms digitize existing processes while preserving the underlying relationships. Coordination becomes slightly more efficient, but the adversarial contracts that make it necessary remain in place.

[035 p] The platforms remain applications that coordinate human activity around traditional buildings, leaving the infrastructure itself unintelligent. Personnel departures remove knowledge, and platform changes make historical information inaccessible. Different platforms also prevent coordination across projects.

[036 p] All three approaches add technology before establishing the foundations it needs. They leave incentives misaligned, data without shared meaning, and recommended actions without autonomous execution. The underlying building remains unintelligent.

[037 p] Building intelligence requires six architectural, economic, and semantic foundations:

[038 li] Shared ontology: A semantic layer where all participants—human and machine—speak the same language and reason about the same concepts

[039 li] Continuous provenance: Cryptographic attestation of every action from manufacturing through operations, creating reliable memory

[040 li] Aligned incentives: Economic structures where all participants profit from collective success rather than individual optimization

[041 li] Transparent value: Automatic attribution of contributions so excellence compounds and mediocrity gets eliminated

[042 li] Collective intelligence: Learning across building networks to continuously improve performance

[043 li] Autonomous agency: Buildings that can act on their own intelligence without constant human intervention

[044 p] Current smart building initiatives concentrate on features and presentation while leaving these infrastructure problems unresolved. Their failure shows why the six foundations must precede those features.

[045 p] The Smart Asset model provides a way to establish those foundations.

[046 h2] Part II: Smart Assets as Physical Infrastructure

[047 h3] Chapter 3: What Are Smart Assets?

[048 p] Smart Buildings extend Smart Assets[2] to physical infrastructure.

[049 p] Traditional financial assets are passive records that banks, brokers, and clearing houses move between accounts: share certificates, bonds, and derivatives. They have no agency, intelligence, or ability to act. Transfers, dividend distributions, compliance checks, and reporting all require external systems operated by institutions.

[050 p] As regulatory complexity and transaction volumes grow, institutions duplicate KYC, compliance, and reconciliation because assets carry no compliance context. Every cross-border transaction requires manual coordination between regulatory regimes, while corporate actions require large operations teams to execute procedures manually. The marginal cost of financial operations never approaches zero because assets remain passive.

[051 p] Smart Assets carry their own intelligence, enforce their own rules, and execute their own operations. Agency moves from the institution alone to the asset itself.

[052 p] A Smart Asset:

[053 li] Knows its own identity through cryptographic primitives that cannot be forged or duplicated

[054 li] Carries its compliance context so it can prove adherence to regulatory requirements without external verification

[055 li] Understands transfer restrictions and automatically enforces rules about who can own it and under what conditions

[056 li] Self-executes corporate actions like dividend distributions based on encoded logic rather than manual processing

[057 li] Maintains complete history of all operations through immutable attestation streams

[058 li] Navigates between jurisdictions seeking optimal regulatory treatment while maintaining continuous compliance

[059 li] Negotiates with institutions by programmatically evaluating service quality and switching providers when better options emerge

[060 p] Mass Protocol provides five programmable primitives that enable this economic coordination:

[061 li] Entities: Legal and natural persons with cryptographic identity and programmatic agency

[062 li] Ownership: Cap tables and token tables with automatic enforcement of equity structures

[063 li] Financial instruments: Bank accounts, wallets, payment rails with programmable money movement

[064 li] Identity: Proof of personhood and portable KYC that travels with users across institutions

[065 li] Consent: Multi-party agreements, board resolutions, and authorization logic that self-executes

[066 p] These primitives compose to enable Smart Assets that operate autonomously across jurisdictional boundaries. An equity token issued through Mass automatically enforces transfer restrictions, distributes dividends based on ownership records, reports to regulators in multiple jurisdictions, and enables fractional ownership with instant settlement—all without continuous human coordination.

[067 p] Value accrues to the infrastructure that enables assets to move between institutions while maintaining compliance, rather than to the assets or institutions themselves. Traditional financial systems concentrate power in institutions that hold assets. Smart Asset systems give that power to assets that choose competing institutions by service quality.

[068 p] Asset mobility creates jurisdictional competition. Jurisdictions must earn the right to host assets through superior service instead of coercive lock-in. Competition then drives regulatory improvement through asset mobility rather than political negotiation.

[069 h3] Chapter 4: Extending Smart Assets to Physical Infrastructure

[070 p] The Smart Asset model maps directly to physical infrastructure. Financial assets embed compliance and identity in digital tokens, and buildings can embed the same primitives.

[071 p] A traditional building requires constant human coordination throughout its lifecycle:

[072 li] Design phase: Architects create models that contractors must interpret and translate

[073 li] Construction phase: Workers build based on incomplete documentation with continuous field modifications

[074 li] Commissioning phase: Operators receive partial information about what was actually built

[075 li] Operations phase: Facility managers react to failures with limited visibility into building state

[076 li] Maintenance phase: Service providers work from outdated documentation and tribal knowledge

[077 li] Transaction phase: Buyers conduct due diligence reconciling fragmented records

[078 li] Modification phase: The cycle repeats with even less reliable information

[079 p] Information degrades at each phase transition. Documentation becomes outdated, personnel changes remove knowledge, and actual building state diverges from the records. Coordination costs compound because nobody has reliable shared information about the building's configuration, operation, or observed behavior.

[080 p] A Smart Building maintains its own state, enforces its own rules, and coordinates its own operations through:

[081 li] Manufacturing provenance: Every component leaves the factory with cryptographic attestation of its specifications, origin, and compliance certifications

[082 li] Construction attestations: Every installation generates signed records of who installed what, when, with what deviations from design

[083 li] Operational memory: All sensor readings, system configurations, and environmental conditions stored in immutable streams

[084 li] Maintenance history: Complete records of all service activities with verification of work completion and quality

[085 li] Financial transparency: All payments, contracts, and obligations encoded in programmable financial instruments

[086 li] Compliance automation: Continuous proof of adherence to regulations through attestation analysis rather than periodic inspection

[087 p] The building carries this intelligence as an intrinsic property. Attestation streams maintain its operational memory, executable compliance rules continuously prove adherence, and programmatic operating rules execute themselves. External documents, regulatory reports, and procedural manuals no longer serve those functions.

[088 p] This transformation requires the same five primitives that enable financial Smart Assets:

[089 p] 1. Entities: The building becomes a legal entity with cryptographic identity. As an active economic actor, it can own assets, execute transactions, enforce rules, and demonstrate compliance.

[090 p] 2. Ownership: Building ownership operates through programmable cap tables. Fractional ownership, revenue sharing, and equity transfers happen automatically based on encoded rules. The building maintains its own ownership registry rather than depending on external title systems.

[091 p] 3. Financial instruments: The building operates its own treasury. Rental contracts, maintenance obligations, utility commitments, and insurance policies are programmable financial instruments that self-execute based on verifiable conditions. When a sensor detects a failure, the building autonomously requests service, authorizes payment, and verifies work completion.

[092 p] 4. Identity: Occupants, operators, service providers, and regulators interact with the building through portable credentials. Access rights, service authorizations, and regulatory permissions are cryptographically managed. A tenant onboards once and carries their credentials to any Smart Building globally.

[093 p] 5. Consent: Building decisions execute through programmatic governance. Fractional owners vote on major changes. Service providers accept or reject contracts. Emergency overrides activate during verified incidents. The building enforces its own governance without external coordination.

[094 p] Financial and physical Smart Assets share the same structure and solve the same problem. They embed intelligence, compliance, and autonomy in assets so they can operate across jurisdictions without constant human coordination.

[095 p] Both depend on standardization. Financial Smart Assets need standard token interfaces to compose, while physical Smart Assets need standardized structures and components. Custom-built buildings cannot be programmable because variability prevents reliable ontology, automated verification, and collective learning. Manufacturing standardization is therefore a prerequisite for building intelligence.

[096 p] Broad Group supplies that manufacturing standardization.

[097 h2] Part III: The Six Foundations for Building Intelligence

[098 h3] Chapter 5: Why These Specific Foundations Matter

[099 p] Making buildings programmable requires six foundations in a specific order. Intelligence depends on architectural, economic, and semantic infrastructure that current building systems lack.

[100 p] The earlier PropTech failures show why these foundations are prerequisites. Each addresses a problem that prevents building intelligence from working reliably. The first is shared meaning across the people and systems that operate a building.

[101 p] Buildings involve thousands of components, systems, contracts, and people, using different languages. Architects, engineers, contractors, and operators each have their own terminology. Machines use multiple incompatible protocols, and legal documents use further terms. Constant translation produces errors, misunderstandings, and coordination failures.

[102 p] The problem is ontological, beyond what file converters can solve: the concept of "wall" means different things to different stakeholders. Architects see design elements with aesthetic properties, while structural engineers see load-bearing components with strength specifications. MEP engineers see utility routes, and contractors see assemblies of materials built with particular techniques. Operators see surfaces requiring maintenance, while occupants see space boundaries.

[103 p] Each perspective is a valid view of the same physical reality, but reconciling them requires constant manual translation. The architect's "wall" becomes the engineer's wall model, then the contractor's wall assembly, then the operator's maintenance schedule. Each translation can introduce errors that compound. By project completion, the as-built wall diverges from the design in ways nobody fully understands.

[104 p] A shared ontology gives "wall" one formal definition, with stakeholder views represented as explicit projections. The architect's aesthetic view, engineer's structural view, contractor's assembly view, and operator's maintenance view derive from the same entity. Changes to it propagate automatically to every view. Because all stakeholders operate on the same object, the model cannot diverge from reality.

[105 p] This requires computational ontology: executable semantics that let machines reason about building concepts as humans do, beyond documentation standards alone. A control command about "wall temperature" must refer to the entity the architect designed, the contractor built, and the operator maintains. Automated operation depends on that shared understanding.

[106 p] Every action on a building—from manufacturing a component to installing a system to adjusting a thermostat—should generate an immutable attestation: who did what, when, where, why, and how. This creates complete provenance: the building's memory of its entire lifecycle from factory to demolition.

[107 p] Traditional construction produces design drawings, shop drawings, installation records, inspection reports, and maintenance logs. These records are incomplete because field modifications, informal repairs, and temporary fixes that become permanent often go undocumented. They are also unreliable: paper records can be falsified, databases can be edited, and dozens of separate systems fail to reconcile their records.

[108 p] The consequences compound over decades. When a system fails, the maintenance technician has no reliable history of prior service. When a modification is planned, the engineer works from outdated documentation that may not reflect actual configuration. When a transaction occurs, the buyer conducts due diligence reconciling contradictory records. At each point, information asymmetry creates risk, cost, and conflict.

[109 p] Cryptographic attestation addresses incompleteness and unreliability together. The installer's device automatically records completed work and signs it cryptographically. Once written to the attestation stream, the record cannot be altered or deleted without detection. Its context includes the unit specifications, configuration, test results, and inspector verification, beyond the statement "HVAC installed".

[110 p] Traditional systems require trust in documentation created by interested parties. Attestation streams eliminate that requirement through cryptographically verifiable records signed by all relevant parties. They reveal complete service histories when components fail, provide definitive timelines for responsibility disputes, and continuously prove compliance.

[111 p] These records also enable collective learning. Across thousands of buildings, attestation streams reveal which components fail most often, which installation techniques perform better, and which operating strategies optimize energy use. Traditional documentation is too unreliable and fragmented for this analysis. Verified streams let buildings learn from each other's experience.

[112 p] A building's experience should inform other buildings. One HVAC failure should change maintenance schedules for similar systems, and an energy optimization technique should propagate across the network. Experiments with new materials should inform all future projects.

[113 p] Buildings currently operate independently, with their own documentation, systems, and locally held knowledge. Universally applicable lessons remain local because there is no way to propagate them. Thousands of buildings repeat the same failures and independently rediscover optimizations they could have inherited.

[114 p] Two gaps cause this isolation: incompatible ontologies prevent comparison, and unreliable provenance prevents verification. If one operator reports 20% energy savings from a control strategy, others need expensive experiments to determine whether the result is real, applicable, or replicable.

[115 p] A shared ontology and attestation streams address both gaps. Operators can compare a building's HVAC performance with thousands of similar buildings using identical equipment. Cryptographic provenance verifies energy savings through recorded evidence rather than self-reports.

[116 p] Learning compounds as the network grows. The first Smart Building relies on its own experience and general industry knowledge. The tenth inherits lessons from the previous nine, while the thousandth benefits from 999 predecessors. Each additional building improves all existing buildings, making the change in capability substantial rather than incremental.

[117 p] Incentive alignment is the most critical foundation because economic interests determine whether the other improvements survive. Participants who profit from dysfunction will preserve it regardless of better tools.

[118 p] Traditional contracts reward that dysfunction. Contractors profit from delays and scope changes, while consultants bill hourly without accountability for outcomes. Developers minimize construction costs and pass operational burdens to buyers. Operators inherit the problems and manage reactively, while insurers pay when failures occur. These incentives follow from the contractual structure itself.

[119 p] Participants consequently hide information, avoid surfacing problems early, and shift blame instead of solving issues. Contracts discourage the coordination that innovation requires. The industry accepts massive waste because eliminating it requires cooperation that existing incentives prevent.

[120 p] The solution changes the economic structure. Participants share the gains from collective performance and share risk backed by insurance. This replaces adversarial contracts that reward individual gains and distribute risk through liability and blame.

[121 p] This is the Instance-Backed Alliance model: all participants in a project—investors, designers, builders, operators, service providers—join a legal entity created specifically for that building. Everyone receives base compensation for their work plus performance bonuses tied to objective building outcomes. If the building is delivered on time, everyone shares the bonus. If the building operates efficiently, everyone shares operational savings. If problems occur, insurance covers losses rather than participants suing each other.

[122 p] Contractors then profit from finishing quickly and building well, designers from efficient building operation, and operators from preventing failures. Delays, changes, billable hours alone, and emergency management cease to drive their rewards. Each participant's interests align with collective success.

[123 p] Performance metrics derive from attestation streams. Cryptographic records verify completion time, sensors measure energy efficiency, and systematic assessments measure occupant satisfaction. The building provides objective performance evidence, eliminating disputes over whether bonuses are deserved.

[124 p] Aligned incentives with mediocre technology produce better results than misaligned incentives with excellent technology. Better tools can make adversarial systems more efficient at preserving their dysfunction. Economic foundations must therefore precede the technology.

[125 p] Aligned incentives and complete provenance make value attribution automatic: the building knows who contributed to its success or failure. Verified contributions allow excellent performers to increase their advantage and poor performers to be removed.

[126 p] In traditional construction, value attribution is opaque. A project succeeds or fails based on aggregate outcome, but individual contributions are invisible. Did the HVAC engineer's clever design save energy, or was efficient operation due to the operator's skill? Did delays result from the contractor's incompetence or from design changes forced by the architect? These questions typically have no definitive answers because reliable evidence doesn't exist.

[127 p] This opacity has two effects. First, excellent performers cannot prove their value and thus cannot command premium compensation. Second, mediocre performers can hide behind aggregate confusion and avoid accountability. The market cannot efficiently price quality because quality cannot be reliably measured.

[128 p] Attestation streams make contribution visible. When an engineer's design achieves 20% energy savings, the attestation stream records the design decisions, their implementation, and the resulting performance. Other projects can verify that this engineer's work reliably produces value. The engineer's reputation becomes quantified and verifiable rather than based on anecdotal claims.

[129 p] Attestation streams also show when a contractor's work causes failures, allowing the market to identify and avoid poor performers. Insurers adjust premiums using verified track records instead of subjective assessments. Objective measurement allows the market to price quality efficiently.

[130 p] Verified results earn high-performing participants a reputation that supports premium pricing on future projects. That pricing attracts the best participants to Instance-Backed Alliances, whose superior outcomes generate stronger attestations. Performance, reputation, and compensation reinforce one another.

[131 p] Poor performers' failures remain visible in attestation streams. Smart Buildings select providers algorithmically using verified performance histories, so poor performers lose market access. Average industry quality rises as excellent participants gain market share and mediocre participants exit.

[132 p] With shared ontology, attestation streams, collective learning, aligned incentives, and transparent value established, buildings can operate as autonomous institutions. They actively manage operations through encoded intelligence instead of waiting for human commands.

[133 p] Autonomy requires all five prerequisites. IoT sensors supply data, digital twins supply simulations, and collaboration software coordinates people. In each case, humans still operate the passive infrastructure. Smart Buildings bring those functions under the building's own authority.

[134 p] A Smart Building holds treasury assets, contracts with service providers, verifies completed work, and authorizes payment. It uses learned intelligence to optimize operations and negotiate better utility rates. It also reports compliance automatically, onboards occupants through programmable identity, and enforces governance rules through programmatic consent.

[135 p] Consider what this means for maintenance. A traditional building detects a failing component through a sensor alert. The facility manager reviews the alert, determines its severity, contacts service providers for quotes, selects a provider, schedules the work, supervises completion, verifies quality, processes payment, and updates documentation. This process takes days or weeks and relies on the manager's judgment, availability, and diligence.

[136 p] A Smart Building's sensors record the failing component in its attestation stream, and its predictive model evaluates failure likelihood and impact. If intervention is warranted, it solicits bids from pre-qualified providers and selects by verified performance history and current availability. It grants access for the scheduled window, then verifies completed work through sensor data and checks quality against its ontology's specifications. Finally, it pays from its treasury and records the complete maintenance history in the attestation stream. The entire process happens automatically in minutes.

[137 p] This autonomy is possible only because all six foundations exist. Shared ontology enables the building to reason about "failing component" the same way service providers do. Attestation streams provide performance history for selecting providers. Collective learning enables predictive models that know when intervention is optimal. Aligned incentives ensure service providers deliver quality work. Transparent value enables automatically verifying work quality. The building operates as an institution because it has the infrastructure institutions require.

[138 h3] Chapter 6: Why These Foundations Must Come First

[139 p] The six foundations have dependencies that determine their implementation order. Each must be established before the capabilities that depend on it can function reliably.

[140 p] First, establish shared ontology. Every subsequent foundation depends on participants understanding the same concepts. Without it, systems cannot coordinate, aggregate data meaningfully, or reason about building concepts.

[141 p] Second, implement attestation streams to provide reliable evidence. Learning, performance-based compensation, and value attribution all depend on verifiable provenance. Attestations replace trust-based coordination with verification.

[142 p] Third, begin collective learning early. Reliable insights require enough attestation data from enough buildings, so this foundation takes time to mature. The sooner buildings learn from one another, the faster their intelligence accumulates.

[143 p] Fourth, align incentives through Instance-Backed Alliance. Legal innovation and stakeholder agreement take time, but this work must precede scaling. Misaligned incentives will otherwise undermine every technical improvement.

[144 p] Fifth, attestation streams and aligned incentives automatically enable transparent value attribution. The building can identify each contribution to its performance, allowing markets to price quality and reputation to reward excellence.

[145 p] Sixth, autonomous operation becomes possible when all previous foundations are established. The building has shared ontology enabling semantic reasoning, attestation streams providing complete memory, collective intelligence from network learning, aligned incentives ensuring good faith participation, and transparent value enabling automatic verification. It can now operate as an institution.

[146 p] Deploying features before these foundations, or trying to establish both together out of order, reliably fails. PropTech companies typically prioritize sensors, dashboards, and optimization algorithms. Improvements remain marginal because the building stays unintelligent, coordination stays manual, and incentives stay misaligned. Users leave after their initial enthusiasm because improvements cannot compound.

[147 p] Smart Buildings establish the foundations through controlled pilots and validate them before deploying dependent features. Early demonstrations are less striking because they test underlying infrastructure. That infrastructure enables improvements to compound instead of producing diminishing returns.

[148 p] Smart Assets provide the precedent for this sequence. Financial infrastructure built on programmable primitives has achieved exponential growth, demonstrating that the approach works. Physical infrastructure can achieve a similar transformation by applying the same pattern correctly.

[150 h3] Chapter 7: HomeOS System Design

[151 p] HomeOS implements the six foundations and supports unlimited extensions through a plug-in architecture. Its operating-system design translates the prerequisites into working layers.

[152 p] Running this architecture requires physical standardization. Custom-built buildings cannot be programmatic because variability prevents reliable ontology, automated verification, and collective learning. Broad Group's modular manufacturing supplies the standardized components and structures HomeOS needs.

[153 p] Every Broad Group module leaves the factory with:

[154 li] Ontological compliance: Components mapped to HomeOS semantic model

[155 li] Embedded instrumentation: Sensors configured to generate standardized attestations

[156 li] Cryptographic identity: Unique identifiers enabling lifecycle tracking

[157 li] Verified specifications: Performance characteristics validated and attested

[158 li] Standard interfaces: Physical and digital connection points matching ontology

[159 p] Standardized manufacturing supports five capabilities:

[160 p] Ontological Consistency: Because modules are standardized, the ontology can precisely describe them. "Broad Group Type A Wall Module" has formal semantic definition that all instances satisfy. This enables reasoning about buildings at scale rather than requiring custom ontologies per building.

[161 p] Automated Verification: Because specifications are known and attested at manufacturing, installation compliance can be verified automatically. The building expects specific components in specific configurations. Installation attestations prove these expectations were satisfied. Deviations are detected immediately rather than discovered later through failures.

[162 p] Collective Learning: Because buildings share standardized components, performance data is directly comparable. When one building's Type A Wall Module exhibits thermal performance, that data informs maintenance schedules for all Type A modules across the network. Learning propagates automatically.

[163 p] Predictive Maintenance: Because failure modes are observed across many instances of standardized components, predictive models become highly accurate. When Module X typically fails after Y operational hours under Z conditions, the building schedules preventive maintenance before failure occurs.

[164 p] Supply Chain Optimization: Because components are standardized and their usage is tracked through attestations, supply chain planning becomes algorithmic. The network knows consumption patterns. Manufacturers optimize production schedules. Just-in-time delivery reduces inventory costs. Components arrive exactly when needed.

[165 p] The Broad Group partnership makes physical structures consistent enough to run software-defined operations at scale. Construction speed and cost also improve dramatically, but programmability is the partnership's purpose.

[166 p] The six foundations describe what must be established. The five HomeOS layers organize the running system, with each depending on the previous layer:

[167 p] Layer 1: Semantic Foundation (Shared Ontology)

[168 p] Every physical component, system, space, contract, and person in a HomeOS building belongs to one ontological framework. Its computational semantics let machines reason about building concepts identically to humans, beyond metadata tagging or categorization.

[169 p] The ontology integrates Compas.dev, an open-source Python framework for computational architecture, and BHoM, the Building and Habitat Object Model. These industry standards let computational architects use their existing concepts while extending them across the full operational lifecycle.

[170 p] The ontology is:

[171 li] Computational: Machines can reason about it, not just parse it

[172 li] Portable: Meaning travels across jurisdictional boundaries unchanged

[173 li] Extensible: New concepts can be added without breaking existing semantics

[174 li] Verifiable: Compliance can be proven against ontological constraints algorithmically

[175 p] When the HVAC system reports "Zone 3 temperature 68°F", the ontology gives every stakeholder the same referent. The architect identifies the design zone, the contractor the physical space, the operator the sensor, the occupant the room, and the regulator the monitored area. These views all refer to the same formally unified entity.

[176 p] Layer 2: Provenance Infrastructure (Attestation Streams)

[177 p] The second layer records the building's history. Every action generates cryptographically signed attestations in immutable streams, which form the building's fundamental record of its existence.

[178 p] Attestations integrate with Mass Protocol's attestation primitives, inheriting their cryptographic security and cross-jurisdictional validity. Each attestation includes:

[179 li] Actor: Who performed the action (cryptographic identity)

[180 li] Action: What was done (semantic concept from Layer 1 ontology)

[181 li] Target: What was acted upon (building entity from ontology)

[182 li] Context: When, where, why, under what conditions

[183 li] Evidence: Sensor data, photos, measurements supporting the attestation

[184 li] Verification: Digital signatures from all relevant parties

[185 p] When a component is installed, the installer's device generates an attestation. Its serial number and specifications are verified against manufacturer records, and AR visualization confirms its location. The inspector verifies code compliance, then all parties sign. The attestation enters the building's stream as a permanent verified record.

[186 p] These streams become the building's authoritative history. They supply service records for maintenance, continuous proof for compliance, and objective evidence for performance evaluation.

[187 p] Layer 3: Economic Foundation

[188 p] The third layer implements aligned incentives. Every HomeOS building is a smart asset, operating through a legal entity created specifically for that building where all participants become members.

[189 p] The building's Mass Protocol smart contracts encode its legal structure:

[190 li] Entity: The building itself is a legal entity with on-chain identity

[191 li] Membership: All participants—investors, designers, builders, operators—are members with equity stakes

[192 li] Base compensation: Everyone receives market rate for their work

[193 li] Performance compensation: Additional payments trigger based on objective outcomes

[194 li] Treasury: The building maintains its own capital and executes its own transactions

[195 li] Insurance: Alliance-level coverage replaces individual liability disputes

Buildings get delivered faster and cheaper while achieving better operational performance. Resources currently consumed by litigation and coordination overhead flow to actual value creation.

[448 p] Smart Buildings also allow physical infrastructure to be managed as software. Operators can add capabilities, optimize operations continuously, debug problems systematically, and propagate improvements automatically.
