Executive Summary

The global construction industry accounts for $12 trillion in annual spending, and real estate comprises $280 trillion in total value. Structural inefficiencies compound across every phase of the building lifecycle. Projects consistently run 30-50% over budget, while operational costs consume 60-80% of lifecycle expenses. Operational state, maintenance history, and compliance status remain fragmented across systems that cannot interoperate.

Buildings are designed, constructed, and operated as static physical objects. They require constant human coordination across disconnected systems and misaligned incentives. Every operational decision, from maintenance scheduling and energy optimization to regulatory reporting, requires manual intervention across parties whose interests fundamentally conflict.

This paper presents Smart Buildings: physical infrastructure operating as autonomous economic entities. Buildings become self-managing institutions and active assets, replacing the passive liabilities and coordination burdens of conventional construction. This requires changing how buildings operate, beyond improving sensors or software.

The proposed institutional model is the Smart Asset.

Smart Assets[1], as defined in Momentum's foundational framework, are autonomous economic entities that carry their complete operational history, understand their regulatory constraints, and execute their own operations without continuous human coordination. They invert the traditional paradigm from institution-centric (where banks hold assets and perform compliance) to asset-centric (where the asset itself performs compliance and navigates between institutions).

The same structure applies to physical infrastructure. Smart Buildings embed the compliance, identity, and operational intelligence that Smart Assets embed in financial instruments. Smart Assets seek optimal regulatory treatment across jurisdictions, while Smart Buildings seek optimal operational frameworks across Special Economic Zones. Both use programmable primitives to execute operations: financial transactions for Smart Assets, and maintenance, energy optimization, and regulatory reporting for Smart Buildings.

This requires solving six problems current smart building initiatives ignore: shared semantic understanding, continuous provenance, collective learning, aligned incentives, transparent value attribution, and autonomous operation. Building intelligence depends on these foundations.

Three capabilities make this possible. Broad Group's 37 years of modular manufacturing standardization provide a programmable physical substrate. Jan Bunge's computational design expertise provides the intelligence layer. Momentum's Mass Protocol infrastructure enables buildings to operate as Smart Assets across jurisdictions.

This paper specifies how these capabilities combine in HomeOS, the first operating system for end-to-end lifecycle management of programmable buildings. It explains why the built environment will move from static structures to autonomous economic institutions.

Part I: The Broken Foundation

Chapter 1: Construction's Coordination Crisis

The construction industry generates more economic value than any sector except finance, yet its coordination mechanisms differ from those of every other modern industry. A typical commercial project involves hundreds of architects, engineers, contractors, suppliers, operators, and regulators. They use different terminology and documentation, work under different incentives, and communicate through manual processes that assume adversarial relationships.

Consider what happens when a mechanical engineer designs an HVAC system. The design exists in specialized software using industry-standard formats like BIM (Building Information Modeling). When construction begins, the contractor translates this design into shop drawings using different software and terminology. During installation, the actual configuration deviates from both design and shop drawings as workers adapt to field conditions. After completion, the building operator receives incomplete documentation using yet another terminology set. When the system requires maintenance years later, the service provider has no reliable record of what was actually installed or how it has been modified.

This is standard practice. Design, as-built, and as-operated records remain disconnected, with no reliable way to reconcile them. Each interface between disciplines requires translation. Every translation introduces errors that compound. By the time a building operates, its configuration only loosely resembles its design, and nobody has complete, accurate documentation of what exists.

Construction projects average 30-50% cost overruns and schedule delays. Building operations consume 60-80% of total lifecycle costs, with 30% of energy wasted through suboptimal control. Reactive maintenance leaves failures to cause disruptions and emergency repairs at 3-5x the cost of scheduled work. Property transactions require months of due diligence to reconcile fragmented records.

Incentives reinforce these failures. Developers profit by minimizing construction costs regardless of operational performance, while contractors profit from extending timelines and charging for changes. Architects and engineers bill hourly with limited accountability for building performance. Operators inherit the resulting problems and manage reactively, while insurers pay when things fail. Contracts structure the entire value chain for conflict: each party seeks maximum personal value while shifting risk and liability to others.

Information asymmetry and blame-shifting displace optimization. Participants avoid disclosing problems early or sharing information transparently, and nobody wants to innovate when contracts discourage the coordination it requires. Despite major advances in materials, software, and construction techniques, the industry has barely improved productivity in fifty years.

Chapter 2: Why the PropTech Industry Has Failed

The PropTech sector has produced dozens of "smart building" companies over the past decade, backed by billions in venture capital. Most have failed or remain marginal. Those that survived typically devolved into property management software—useful operational tools, but not a new class of infrastructure.

The three approaches failed for different reasons.

Limits of Adding Sensors

The first wave added sensors to otherwise passive buildings. Companies deployed IoT networks for occupancy detection, temperature monitoring, and energy measurement. Machine learning models analyzed the resulting large volumes of data to optimize operations.

Sensors leave the underlying coordination problems intact: adversarial contracts, fragmented documentation, and misaligned incentives. They accelerate activity within those constraints. Operators may decline optimization recommendations because of other priorities. Training data may misrepresent the actual building configuration, and the metrics being optimized may conflict with stakeholder interests.

These systems observe building operations and suggest improvements. They cannot act autonomously because they lack agency, authority, and integration with the decision-making structures that control operations. They function as dashboards rather than operating systems.

Digital Twins Diverge from Building State

The second wave used comprehensive building models to support intelligence. Digital twins simulate building state and allow virtual experiments before physical changes. They demonstrated that computational modeling could handle building complexity, a significant technical achievement.

The digital twin remains separate from the physical building. Its simulation attempts to track reality but has no causal authority over it. Actual building state diverges from the model immediately and continuously, with no mechanism to reconcile the two. The technically impressive model becomes operationally irrelevant.

Digital twins are also typically created at design time and never updated as the building evolves. They capture the architect's intent rather than the contractor's execution or the operator's modifications. Their resemblance to the actual building becomes superficial, so decisions based on them have an uncertain relationship to physical outcomes.

Better information also leaves incentive misalignment intact. Contractors still profit from delays, and operators still manage reactively under the same adversarial contracts. Information asymmetry and blame-shifting continue despite better visualizations.

Coordination Software Preserves Existing Relationships

The third wave focused on collaboration—software platforms where architects, engineers, contractors, and operators could share information and coordinate activities. These tools improved communication and reduced some coordination friction through centralized document management and task tracking.

These platforms digitize existing processes while preserving the underlying relationships. Coordination becomes slightly more efficient, but the adversarial contracts that make it necessary remain in place.

The platforms remain applications that coordinate human activity around traditional buildings, leaving the infrastructure itself unintelligent. Personnel departures remove knowledge, and platform changes make historical information inaccessible. Different platforms also prevent coordination across projects.

The Required Foundations

All three approaches add technology before establishing the foundations it needs. They leave incentives misaligned, data without shared meaning, and recommended actions without autonomous execution. The underlying building remains unintelligent.

Building intelligence requires six architectural, economic, and semantic foundations:

  1. Shared ontology: A semantic layer where all participants—human and machine—speak the same language and reason about the same concepts
  2. Continuous provenance: Cryptographic attestation of every action from manufacturing through operations, creating reliable memory
  3. Aligned incentives: Economic structures where all participants profit from collective success rather than individual optimization
  4. Transparent value: Automatic attribution of contributions so excellence compounds and mediocrity gets eliminated
  5. Collective intelligence: Learning across building networks to continuously improve performance
  6. Autonomous agency: Buildings that can act on their own intelligence without constant human intervention

Current smart building initiatives concentrate on features and presentation while leaving these infrastructure problems unresolved. Their failure shows why the six foundations must precede those features.

The Smart Asset model provides a way to establish those foundations.

Part II: Smart Assets as Physical Infrastructure

Chapter 3: What Are Smart Assets?

Smart Buildings extend Smart Assets[2] to physical infrastructure.

Traditional financial assets are passive records that banks, brokers, and clearing houses move between accounts: share certificates, bonds, and derivatives. They have no agency, intelligence, or ability to act. Transfers, dividend distributions, compliance checks, and reporting all require external systems operated by institutions.

As regulatory complexity and transaction volumes grow, every institution duplicates KYC, compliance, and reconciliation because assets carry no compliance context. Every cross-border transaction requires manual coordination between regulatory regimes, while every corporate action requires large operations teams to execute procedures manually. The marginal cost of financial operations never approaches zero because assets remain passive.

Smart Assets carry their own intelligence, enforce their own rules, and execute their own operations. Agency moves from the institution alone to the asset itself.

A Smart Asset:

  • Knows its own identity through cryptographic primitives that cannot be forged or duplicated
  • Carries its compliance context so it can prove adherence to regulatory requirements without external verification
  • Understands transfer restrictions and automatically enforces rules about who can own it and under what conditions
  • Self-executes corporate actions like dividend distributions based on encoded logic rather than manual processing
  • Maintains complete history of all operations through immutable attestation streams
  • Navigates between jurisdictions seeking optimal regulatory treatment while maintaining continuous compliance
  • Negotiates with institutions by programmatically evaluating service quality and switching providers when better options emerge

Mass Protocol provides five programmable primitives that enable this economic coordination:

  1. Entities: Legal and natural persons with cryptographic identity and programmatic agency
  2. Ownership: Cap tables and token tables with automatic enforcement of equity structures
  3. Financial instruments: Bank accounts, wallets, payment rails with programmable money movement
  4. Identity: Proof of personhood and portable KYC that travels with users across institutions
  5. Consent: Multi-party agreements, board resolutions, and authorization logic that self-executes

These primitives compose to enable Smart Assets that operate autonomously across jurisdictional boundaries. An equity token issued through Mass automatically enforces transfer restrictions, distributes dividends based on ownership records, reports to regulators in multiple jurisdictions, and enables fractional ownership with instant settlement—all without continuous human coordination.

Value accrues to the infrastructure that enables assets to move between institutions while maintaining compliance, rather than to the assets or institutions themselves. Traditional financial systems concentrate power in institutions that hold assets. Smart Asset systems give that power to assets that choose competing institutions by service quality.

Asset mobility creates jurisdictional competition. Jurisdictions must earn the right to host assets through superior service instead of coercive lock-in. Competition then drives regulatory improvement through asset mobility rather than political negotiation.

Chapter 4: Extending Smart Assets to Physical Infrastructure

The Smart Asset model maps directly to physical infrastructure. Financial assets embed compliance and identity in digital tokens, and buildings can embed the same primitives.

A traditional building requires constant human coordination throughout its lifecycle:

  • Design phase: Architects create models that contractors must interpret and translate
  • Construction phase: Workers build based on incomplete documentation with continuous field modifications
  • Commissioning phase: Operators receive partial information about what was actually built
  • Operations phase: Facility managers react to failures with limited visibility into building state
  • Maintenance phase: Service providers work from outdated documentation and tribal knowledge
  • Transaction phase: Buyers conduct due diligence reconciling fragmented records
  • Modification phase: The cycle repeats with even less reliable information

Information degrades at each phase transition. Documentation becomes outdated, personnel changes remove knowledge, and actual building state diverges from the records. Coordination costs compound because nobody has reliable shared information about the building's configuration, operation, or observed behavior.

A Smart Building maintains its own state, enforces its own rules, and coordinates its own operations through:

  • Manufacturing provenance: Every component leaves the factory with cryptographic attestation of its specifications, origin, and compliance certifications
  • Construction attestations: Every installation generates signed records of who installed what, when, with what deviations from design
  • Operational memory: All sensor readings, system configurations, and environmental conditions stored in immutable streams
  • Maintenance history: Complete records of all service activities with verification of work completion and quality
  • Financial transparency: All payments, contracts, and obligations encoded in programmable financial instruments
  • Compliance automation: Continuous proof of adherence to regulations through attestation analysis rather than periodic inspection

The building carries this intelligence as an intrinsic property. Attestation streams maintain its operational memory, executable compliance rules continuously prove adherence, and programmatic operating rules execute themselves.

This transformation requires the same five primitives that enable financial Smart Assets:

1. Entities: The building becomes a legal entity with cryptographic identity. As an active economic actor, it can own assets, execute transactions, enforce rules, and demonstrate compliance.

2. Ownership: Building ownership operates through programmable cap tables. Fractional ownership, revenue sharing, and equity transfers happen automatically based on encoded rules. The building maintains its own ownership registry rather than depending on external title systems.

3. Financial instruments: The building operates its own treasury. Rental contracts, maintenance obligations, utility commitments, and insurance policies are programmable financial instruments that self-execute based on verifiable conditions. When a sensor detects a failure, the building autonomously requests service, authorizes payment, and verifies work completion.

4. Identity: Occupants, operators, service providers, and regulators interact with the building through portable credentials. Access rights, service authorizations, and regulatory permissions are cryptographically managed. A tenant onboards once and carries their credentials to any Smart Building globally.

5. Consent: Building decisions execute through programmatic governance. Fractional owners vote on major changes. Service providers accept or reject contracts. Emergency overrides activate during verified incidents. The building enforces its own governance without external coordination.

Financial and physical Smart Assets share the same structure and solve the same problem. They embed intelligence, compliance, and autonomy in assets so they can operate across jurisdictions without constant human coordination.

Both depend on standardization. Financial Smart Assets need standard token interfaces to compose, while physical Smart Assets need standardized structures and components. Custom-built buildings cannot be programmable because variability prevents reliable ontology, automated verification, and collective learning. Manufacturing standardization is therefore a prerequisite for building intelligence.

Broad Group supplies that manufacturing standardization.

Part III: The Six Foundations for Building Intelligence

Chapter 5: Why These Specific Foundations Matter

Making buildings programmable requires six foundations in a specific order. Intelligence depends on architectural, economic, and semantic infrastructure that current building systems lack.

The earlier PropTech failures show why these foundations are prerequisites. Each addresses a problem that prevents building intelligence from working reliably. The first is shared meaning across the people and systems that operate a building.

Foundation 1: Shared Semantic Understanding

Buildings involve thousands of components, systems, contracts, and people, using different languages. Architects, engineers, contractors, and operators each have their own terminology. Machines use multiple incompatible protocols, and legal documents use further terms. Constant translation produces errors, misunderstandings, and coordination failures.

The problem is ontological, beyond what file converters can solve: the concept of "wall" means different things to different stakeholders. Architects see design elements with aesthetic properties, while structural engineers see load-bearing components with strength specifications. MEP engineers see utility routes, and contractors see assemblies of materials built with particular techniques. Operators see surfaces requiring maintenance, while occupants see space boundaries.

Each perspective is a valid view of the same physical reality. When these views cannot be formally reconciled, coordination requires constant manual translation. The architect's "wall" becomes the engineer's wall model, then the contractor's wall assembly, then the operator's maintenance schedule. Each translation can introduce errors that compound. By project completion, the as-built wall diverges from the design in ways nobody fully understands.

A shared ontology gives "wall" one formal definition, with stakeholder views represented as explicit projections. The architect's aesthetic view, engineer's structural view, contractor's assembly view, and operator's maintenance view derive from the same entity. Changes to it propagate automatically to every view. Because all stakeholders operate on the same object, the model cannot diverge from reality.

This requires computational ontology: executable semantics that let machines reason about building concepts as humans do, beyond documentation standards alone. For a control command about "wall temperature," the system must identify the entity the architect designed, the contractor built, and the operator maintains. Automated operation depends on that shared understanding.

Foundation 2: Continuous Cryptographic Provenance

Every action on a building—from manufacturing a component to installing a system to adjusting a thermostat—should generate an immutable attestation: who did what, when, where, why, and how. This creates complete provenance: the building's memory of its entire lifecycle from factory to demolition.

Traditional construction produces design drawings, shop drawings, installation records, inspection reports, and maintenance logs. These records are incomplete because field modifications, informal repairs, and temporary fixes that become permanent often go undocumented. They are also unreliable: paper records can be falsified, databases can be edited, and dozens of separate systems fail to reconcile their records.

The consequences compound over decades. When a system fails, the maintenance technician has no reliable history of prior service. When a modification is planned, the engineer works from outdated documentation that may not reflect actual configuration. When a transaction occurs, the buyer conducts due diligence reconciling contradictory records. At each point, information asymmetry creates risk, cost, and conflict.

Cryptographic attestation solves incompleteness and unreliability together. Attestations are generated automatically as actions occur: the installer's device records completed work and signs it cryptographically. Once written to the attestation stream, the record cannot be altered or deleted without detection. Its complete context includes unit specifications, configuration, test results, and inspector verification, beyond the statement "HVAC installed".

Traditional systems require trust in documentation created by interested parties. Attestation streams eliminate that requirement through cryptographically verifiable records signed by all relevant parties. They reveal complete service histories when components fail, provide definitive timelines for responsibility disputes, and continuously prove compliance.

These records also enable collective learning. Across thousands of buildings, attestation streams reveal which components fail most often, which installation techniques perform better, and which operating strategies optimize energy use. Traditional documentation is too unreliable and fragmented for this analysis. Verified streams let buildings learn from each other's experience.

Foundation 3: Collective Learning from Network Experience

Buildings should inherit lessons from one another. One HVAC failure should inform maintenance schedules for all similar buildings, and an energy optimization technique should propagate across the network. The results of experiments with new materials should be available to all future projects.

Buildings currently operate independently, with their own documentation, systems, and locally held knowledge. Universally applicable lessons remain local because there is no way to propagate them. Thousands of buildings repeat the same failures and independently rediscover optimizations they could have inherited.

Two gaps cause this isolation: incompatible ontologies prevent comparison, and unreliable provenance prevents verification. If one operator reports 20% energy savings from a control strategy, others need expensive experiments to determine whether the result is real, applicable, or replicable.

A shared ontology and attestation streams solve both problems. Operators can compare a building's HVAC performance with thousands of similar buildings using identical equipment. Cryptographic provenance verifies energy savings through recorded evidence rather than self-reports.

Learning compounds as the network grows. The first Smart Building relies on its own experience and general industry knowledge. The tenth inherits lessons from the previous nine, while the thousandth benefits from 999 predecessors. Each additional building improves all existing buildings, making the change in capability substantial rather than incremental.

Foundation 4: Economic Alignment Through Shared Performance Incentives

Incentive alignment is the most critical foundation because economic interests determine whether the other improvements survive. Participants who profit from dysfunction will preserve it regardless of better tools.

Traditional contracts reward that dysfunction. Contractors profit from delays and scope changes, while consultants bill hourly without accountability for outcomes. Developers minimize construction costs and pass operational burdens to buyers. Operators inherit the problems and manage reactively, while insurers pay when failures occur. These incentives follow from the contractual structure itself.

Participants consequently hide information, avoid surfacing problems early, and shift blame instead of solving issues. Contracts discourage the coordination that innovation requires. The industry accepts massive waste because eliminating it requires cooperation that existing incentives prevent.

The solution changes the economic structure. Participants share the gains from collective performance and share risk backed by insurance. This replaces adversarial contracts that reward individual gains and distribute risk through liability and blame.

This is the Instance-Backed Alliance model: all participants in a project—investors, designers, builders, operators, service providers—join a legal entity created specifically for that building. Everyone receives base compensation for their work plus performance bonuses tied to objective building outcomes. If the building is delivered on time, everyone shares the bonus. If the building operates efficiently, everyone shares operational savings. If problems occur, insurance covers losses rather than participants suing each other.

Contractors then profit from finishing quickly and building well, designers from efficient building operation, and operators from preventing failures. Delays, changes, billable hours alone, and emergency management cease to drive their rewards. Each participant's interests align with collective success.

Performance metrics derive from attestation streams. Cryptographic records verify completion time, sensors measure energy efficiency, and systematic assessments measure occupant satisfaction. The building provides objective performance evidence, eliminating disputes over whether bonuses are deserved.

Aligned incentives with mediocre technology produce better results than misaligned incentives with excellent technology. Better tools in adversarial systems make dysfunction more efficient. The economic foundations must be fixed before technology can deliver value.

Foundation 5: Transparent Value Attribution

Aligned incentives and complete provenance make value attribution automatic: the building knows exactly who contributed what to its success or failure. Verified contributions allow excellent performers to increase their advantage and poor performers to be removed.

In traditional construction, value attribution is opaque. A project succeeds or fails based on aggregate outcome, but individual contributions are invisible. Did the HVAC engineer's clever design save energy, or was efficient operation due to the operator's skill? Did delays result from the contractor's incompetence or from design changes forced by the architect? These questions typically have no definitive answers because reliable evidence doesn't exist.

This opacity has two effects. First, excellent performers cannot prove their value and thus cannot command premium compensation. Second, mediocre performers can hide behind aggregate confusion and avoid accountability. The market cannot efficiently price quality because quality cannot be reliably measured.

Attestation streams make contribution visible. When an engineer's design achieves 20% energy savings, the attestation stream records the design decisions, their implementation, and the resulting performance. Other projects can verify that this engineer's work reliably produces value. The engineer's reputation becomes quantified and verifiable rather than based on anecdotal claims.

Attestation streams also show when a contractor's work causes failures, allowing the market to identify and avoid poor performers. Insurers adjust premiums using verified track records instead of subjective assessments. Objective measurement allows the market to price quality efficiently.

Verified results earn high-performing participants a reputation that supports premium pricing on future projects. That pricing attracts the best participants to Instance-Backed Alliances, whose superior outcomes generate stronger attestations. Performance, reputation, and compensation reinforce one another.

Poor performers' failures remain visible in attestation streams. Smart Buildings select providers algorithmically using verified performance histories, so poor performers lose market access. Average industry quality rises as excellent participants gain market share and mediocre participants exit.

Foundation 6: Buildings as Autonomous Institutions

With shared ontology, attestation streams, collective learning, aligned incentives, and transparent value established, buildings can operate as autonomous institutions. They actively manage operations through encoded intelligence instead of waiting for human commands.

Autonomy requires all five prerequisites. IoT sensors supply data, digital twins supply simulations, and collaboration software coordinates people. In each case, humans still operate the passive infrastructure. Smart Buildings bring those functions under the building's own authority.

A Smart Building holds treasury assets, contracts with service providers, verifies completed work, and authorizes payment. It uses learned intelligence to optimize operations. It also negotiates better utility rates, reports compliance to regulators automatically, onboards occupants through programmable identity, and enforces governance rules through programmatic consent.

Consider what this means for maintenance. A traditional building detects a failing component through a sensor alert. The facility manager reviews the alert, determines its severity, contacts service providers for quotes, selects a provider, schedules the work, supervises completion, verifies quality, processes payment, and updates documentation. This process takes days or weeks and relies on the manager's judgment, availability, and diligence.

A Smart Building's sensors record the failing component in its attestation stream, and its predictive model evaluates failure likelihood and impact. If intervention is warranted, it solicits bids from pre-qualified providers and selects by verified performance history and current availability. It grants access credentials for the scheduled window, then uses sensor data to verify completed work and checks quality against its ontology's specifications. Finally, it pays from its treasury and records the complete maintenance history in the attestation stream. The entire process happens automatically in minutes.

This autonomy is possible only because all six foundations exist. Shared ontology enables the building to reason about "failing component" the same way service providers do. Attestation streams provide performance history for selecting providers. Collective learning enables predictive models that know when intervention is optimal. Aligned incentives ensure service providers deliver quality work. Transparent value enables automatically verifying work quality. The building operates as an institution because it has the infrastructure institutions require.

Chapter 6: Why These Foundations Must Come First

The six foundations have dependencies that determine their implementation order. Each must be established before the capabilities that depend on it can function reliably.

First, establish shared ontology. Every subsequent foundation depends on participants understanding the same concepts. Without it, systems cannot coordinate, aggregate data meaningfully, or reason about building concepts.

Second, implement attestation streams to provide reliable evidence. Learning, performance-based compensation, and value attribution all depend on verifiable provenance. Attestations replace trust-based coordination with verification.

Third, begin collective learning early. Reliable insights require enough attestation data from enough buildings, so this foundation takes time to mature. The sooner buildings learn from one another, the faster their intelligence accumulates.

Fourth, align incentives through Instance-Backed Alliance. Legal innovation and stakeholder agreement take time, but this work must precede scaling. Misaligned incentives will otherwise undermine every technical improvement.

Fifth, attestation streams and aligned incentives automatically enable transparent value attribution. The building can identify each contribution to its performance, allowing markets to price quality and reputation to reward excellence.

Sixth, autonomous operation becomes possible when all previous foundations are established. The building has shared ontology enabling semantic reasoning, attestation streams providing complete memory, collective intelligence from network learning, aligned incentives ensuring good faith participation, and transparent value enabling automatic verification. It can now operate as an institution.

Establishing foundations out of order or in parallel with feature deployment reliably fails. PropTech companies typically prioritize sensors, dashboards, and optimization algorithms. Improvements remain marginal because the building stays unintelligent, coordination stays manual, and incentives stay misaligned. Users leave after their initial enthusiasm because improvements cannot compound.

Smart Buildings establish the foundations through controlled pilots and validate them before deploying dependent features. Early demonstrations are less striking because they test underlying infrastructure. That infrastructure enables improvements to compound instead of producing diminishing returns.

Smart Assets provide the precedent for this sequence. Financial infrastructure built on programmable primitives has achieved exponential growth, demonstrating that the approach works. Physical infrastructure can achieve a similar transformation by applying the same pattern correctly.

Part IV: Technical Architecture

Chapter 7: HomeOS System Design

HomeOS implements the six foundations and supports unlimited extensions through a plug-in architecture. Its operating-system design translates the prerequisites into working layers.

Integration with Broad Group Manufacturing

Running this architecture requires physical standardization. Custom-built buildings cannot be programmatic because variability prevents reliable ontology, automated verification, and collective learning. Broad Group's modular manufacturing supplies the standardized components and structures HomeOS needs.

Every Broad Group module leaves the factory with:

  • Ontological compliance: Components mapped to HomeOS semantic model
  • Embedded instrumentation: Sensors configured to generate standardized attestations
  • Cryptographic identity: Unique identifiers enabling lifecycle tracking
  • Verified specifications: Performance characteristics validated and attested
  • Standard interfaces: Physical and digital connection points matching ontology

Standardized manufacturing supports five capabilities:

Ontological Consistency: Because modules are standardized, the ontology can precisely describe them. "Broad Group Type A Wall Module" has formal semantic definition that all instances satisfy. This enables reasoning about buildings at scale rather than requiring custom ontologies per building.

Automated Verification: Because specifications are known and attested at manufacturing, installation compliance can be verified automatically. The building expects specific components in specific configurations. Installation attestations prove these expectations were satisfied. Deviations are detected immediately rather than discovered later through failures.

Collective Learning: Because buildings share standardized components, performance data is directly comparable. When one building's Type A Wall Module exhibits thermal performance, that data informs maintenance schedules for all Type A modules across the network. Learning propagates automatically.

Predictive Maintenance: Because failure modes are observed across many instances of standardized components, predictive models become highly accurate. When Module X typically fails after Y operational hours under Z conditions, the building schedules preventive maintenance before failure occurs.

Supply Chain Optimization: Because components are standardized and their usage is tracked through attestations, supply chain planning becomes algorithmic. The network knows consumption patterns. Manufacturers optimize production schedules. Just-in-time delivery reduces inventory costs. Components arrive exactly when needed.

The Broad Group partnership makes physical structures consistent enough to run software-defined operations at scale. Construction speed and cost also improve dramatically, but programmability is the partnership's purpose.

The Layered Architecture

The six foundations describe what must be established. The five HomeOS layers organize the running system, with each depending on the previous layer:

Layer 1: Semantic Foundation (Shared Ontology)

Every physical component, system, space, contract, and person in a HomeOS building belongs to one ontological framework. Its computational semantics let machines reason about building concepts identically to humans, beyond metadata tagging or categorization.

The ontology integrates with Compas.dev, an open-source Python framework for computational architecture, and BHoM, the Building and Habitat Object Model. These industry standards let computational architects use their existing concepts while extending them across the full operational lifecycle.

The ontology is:

  • Computational: Machines can reason about it, not just parse it
  • Portable: Meaning travels across jurisdictional boundaries unchanged
  • Extensible: New concepts can be added without breaking existing semantics
  • Verifiable: Compliance can be proven against ontological constraints algorithmically

When the HVAC system reports "Zone 3 temperature 68°F", the ontology gives every stakeholder the same referent. The architect identifies the design zone, the contractor the physical space, the operator the sensor, the occupant the room, and the regulator the monitored area. These views all refer to the same formally unified entity.

Layer 2: Provenance Infrastructure (Attestation Streams)

The second layer records the building's history. Every action generates cryptographically signed attestations in immutable streams, which form the building's fundamental record of its existence.

Attestations integrate with Mass Protocol's attestation primitives, inheriting their cryptographic security and cross-jurisdictional validity. Each attestation includes:

  • Actor: Who performed the action (cryptographic identity)
  • Action: What was done (semantic concept from Layer 1 ontology)
  • Target: What was acted upon (building entity from ontology)
  • Context: When, where, why, under what conditions
  • Evidence: Sensor data, photos, measurements supporting the attestation
  • Verification: Digital signatures from all relevant parties

When a component is installed, the installer's device generates an attestation. The component's serial number and specifications are verified against manufacturer records, and AR visualization confirms its location. The inspector verifies code compliance, then all parties sign. The attestation enters the building's stream as a permanent verified record.

These streams become the building's authoritative history. They supply complete service histories for maintenance, continuous proof for compliance, and objective evidence for performance evaluation.

Layer 3: Economic Foundation

The third layer implements aligned incentives. Every HomeOS building is a smart asset, operating through a legal entity created specifically for that building where all participants become members.

The building's Mass Protocol smart contracts encode its legal structure:

  • Entity: The building itself is a legal entity with on-chain identity
  • Membership: All participants—investors, designers, builders, operators—are members with equity stakes
  • Base compensation: Everyone receives market rate for their work
  • Performance compensation: Additional payments trigger based on objective outcomes
  • Treasury: The building maintains its own capital and executes its own transactions
  • Insurance: Alliance-level coverage replaces individual liability disputes
  • Governance: Building decisions execute through programmatic consensus mechanisms

Attestation streams from Layer 2 supply performance metrics, evaluated against Layer 1's ontological specifications. Cryptographic records verify completion time, continuous measurements establish energy efficiency, and transparent records track maintenance costs. Systematic assessments establish occupant satisfaction. This objective evidence eliminates disputes.

When performance triggers occur, the building's treasury automatically distributes compensation to members according to encoded rules. It manages its finances without invoices, approval workflows, or payment delays.

Layer 4: Intelligence Layer (Optimization and Learning)

With the first three layers established, the fourth can optimize building operations and learn from the network.

The intelligence layer includes:

  • Energy optimization: Algorithms coordinating HVAC, lighting, and occupancy
  • Predictive maintenance: Models forecasting component failures before they occur
  • Space utilization: Analytics informing layout and capacity planning
  • Financial optimization: Managing utility contracts, service procurement, treasury operations
  • Collective learning: Insights from network improving all buildings

This intelligence works because:

  • It reasons about shared ontology (Layer 1) so insights apply across buildings
  • It learns from attestation streams (Layer 2) so training data is verified and complete
  • It optimizes aligned incentives (Layer 3) so improvements benefit all stakeholders
  • It compounds across network through collective learning

When the building detects changed occupancy patterns, energy optimization adjusts HVAC schedules and predictive maintenance moves service to the new low-occupancy periods. Space utilization proposes reconfiguring underused areas, while financial optimization renegotiates utility contracts for the new load profiles. Collective learning propagates these responses to similar buildings, so one building's adaptation benefits the others.

Layer 5: Extension Marketplace (Plug-in Ecosystem)

The top layer enables unlimited extensibility through plug-in architecture. Third-party developers build on the foundation layers to provide specialized services.

The marketplace enables:

  • Service discovery: Buildings find providers for specialized needs
  • Performance verification: Attestation streams prove service quality
  • Automatic procurement: Buildings solicit bids and select providers algorithmically
  • Reputation effects: Excellent providers capture more market share through verified track records
  • Network effects: More buildings increase marketplace liquidity, attracting better providers

A building needing landscaping automatically solicits bids from network providers with verified track records and evaluates proposals using attestation-proven performance. It selects the optimal provider by price, quality, and availability, then grants time-limited access credentials. Sensor data and photographic evidence verify completion before the building pays from its treasury. The verified outcome updates the landscaper's reputation, and the building records the complete transaction in its attestation stream.

In this "operating system" model, HomeOS supplies core infrastructure in Layers 1-4, while the ecosystem supplies unlimited extensions in Layer 5. As iOS supports millions of apps, HomeOS supports an entire service economy around intelligent buildings.

Chapter 11: Why Existing Players Cannot Respond

Current market participants face structural barriers preventing them from adopting the Smart Building model.

IoT and Building Management System Vendors

Companies like Johnson Controls, Honeywell, Siemens cannot evolve to HomeOS because:

Business model conflicts: These vendors profit from proprietary systems and vendor lock-in. Shared ontology threatens differentiation, while open platforms commoditize their offerings. Their incentives favor continued fragmentation.

Technical debt: Their installed base operates on legacy protocols that cannot interoperate with modern semantic systems. Retrofitting millions of existing installations is economically infeasible.

Organizational structure: These companies are organized around product lines (HVAC, lighting, security) rather than integrated building operations. Creating shared ontology requires cross-product coordination their structures prevent.

HomeOS integrates with their hardware while providing the intelligence layer they cannot. Their sensors become components in HomeOS buildings, but the vendors themselves cannot become HomeOS because the transformation would destroy their existing business.

PropTech and Property Management Platforms

Companies like Yardi, AppFolio, Buildium cannot evolve to HomeOS because:

No access to foundations: These software applications depend on traditional buildings. Establishing shared ontology requires changing the construction industry, generating attestations requires access to building systems, and implementing Instance-Backed Alliance requires influence over legal structures. The platforms lack all three capabilities.

Wrong customer: Their customers are property managers, whose role exists because buildings need manual coordination. If HomeOS succeeds and buildings manage themselves, that customer base disappears.

Wrong architecture: These applications coordinate human activity around buildings that require human operation. Their value proposition depends on that requirement, preventing a shift to the infrastructure needed for autonomous operation.

If HomeOS succeeds, property-management software shrinks dramatically as buildings coordinate their own operations. PropTech serves the remaining edge cases.

Digital Twin and BIM Platforms

Companies like Bentley Systems, Autodesk, Dassault Systèmes cannot evolve to HomeOS because:

Design-time focus: These vendors excel at modeling intended buildings but fail to ensure that construction and operation match the design. Their tools leave the "as-designed", "as-built", and "as-operated" states disconnected.

No operational integration: Digital twins remain simulations separate from physical buildings. They can observe and recommend, but cannot act or execute. They permanently remain models of operations rather than carrying them out.

No incentive solution: These vendors assume traditional contracts will execute design intent faithfully. Resolving incentive misalignment lies outside their scope and expertise.

These companies provide valuable design tools, and Bentley Systems is a potential partner. Its tools can provide the HomeOS front-end, with HomeOS making the designs operational. The vendors cannot become HomeOS because they lack a path from simulation to autonomous operation.

Chapter 13: Why This Succeeds Where Others Failed

The implementation sequence addresses the failures identified earlier: premature features, misaligned incentives, isolated operations, and custom construction that cannot scale.

Failure Mode 1: Features Without Foundations

Most PropTech deployed features before establishing ontology, attestation streams, and aligned incentives. Gains remained marginal, and users left after their initial enthusiasm because improvements could not compound. This makes foundation validation the first implementation test.

HomeOS uses controlled pilots to validate those foundations before deploying dependent sensors, dashboards, and optimization algorithms. Early demonstrations are less striking because they test underlying infrastructure. Establishing that infrastructure lets subsequent improvements compound instead of yielding diminishing returns.

Failure Mode 2: Technology Without Economics

The next test concerns adoption. Many initiatives achieved technical excellence but stalled because existing contracts prevented stakeholders from capturing value. Established revenue interests blocked innovation even when the technology worked perfectly.

HomeOS solves economics first through Instance-Backed Alliance. Collective success becomes profitable for every participant, and everyone gains new revenue streams without threatening anyone's existing business model. This removes the adoption friction caused by misaligned incentives.

Failure Mode 3: Isolated Solutions Without Network Effects

Scaling also requires learning to transfer between projects. Previous platforms isolated each building's optimization, so every project started again and the network effects needed for defensibility failed to develop.

HomeOS establishes the network from the first deployment through:

  • Shared ontology enabling building comparison
  • Attestation streams enabling collective learning
  • Instance-Backed Alliance creating professional community
  • Service marketplace generating liquidity effects
  • Developer ecosystem creating capability acceleration

These relationships let network effects compound from the beginning, before the platform reaches scale.

Failure Mode 4: Custom Approaches Without Standardization

Physical replication is the final test. Many earlier initiatives used custom design and construction, leaving each building unique and preventing reliable automation, meaningful comparison, or collective learning. They produced individual demonstrations that could not scale economically.

The Broad Group partnership supplies the standardization required for:

  • Consistent ontology across buildings
  • Automated verification through known specifications
  • Collective learning from comparable data
  • Manufacturing economies from volume production
  • Predictable outcomes from validated designs

Standardization therefore makes programmability and economic replication possible.

Chapter 14: Strategic Implications

These implementation choices have consequences beyond building operations. Smart Buildings change physical infrastructure on a scale comparable to cryptographic primitives' effect on financial markets.

Jurisdictional Competition Intensifies

When buildings operate as Smart Assets navigating between jurisdictions, competition for capital shifts from coercive lock-in to service quality. Jurisdictions that provide superior infrastructure, streamlined compliance, and supportive regulation attract building deployments. Those that maintain traditional friction lose out.

Buildings will not deploy where compliance is absent because they need regulatory frameworks to operate legally. They will seek jurisdictions that enable automation, recognize attestation streams, and support innovation. Jurisdictions compete on how efficiently they serve Smart Assets rather than their ability to prevent the assets' departure.

Momentum's SEZ network accelerates this competition as zones seek HomeOS deployments. Successful zones provide evidence that influences traditional jurisdictions. Infrastructure innovation then gradually changes global regulation, rather than relying on political negotiation.

Real Estate Markets Achieve Liquidity

Traditional real estate remains illiquid because verification costs are enormous. Buyers conduct months of due diligence reconciling fragmented records. Lenders require extensive inspection and appraisal. Insurance demands detailed disclosure and investigation. These transaction costs make real estate nearly impossible to trade at scale.

Smart Buildings solve this through attestation streams that provide complete verified history. Buyers can inspect decades of operational data in minutes. Lenders can analyze cash flows and performance trends algorithmically. Insurance can price risk based on verified maintenance records rather than subjective assessment.

This enables tokenization with genuine liquidity. When building history is verifiable and performance is transparent, fractional ownership becomes practical. Secondary markets can operate with instant settlement. Real estate approaches the liquidity of financial assets.

Liquidity in a $280 trillion asset class creates trillions in new economic activity. Homeowners can access equity without selling, while investors can diversify real-estate exposure efficiently. More precisely measurable returns improve capital allocation.

Construction Industry Transforms

When all participants profit from collective success, Instance-Backed Alliance changes construction work. Collaboration replaces conflict, information sharing replaces concealment, and early problem identification replaces blame-shifting. Innovation becomes rewarded rather than punished.

The talent dynamics shift dramatically. Excellent performers capture premium compensation through verified track records. They preferentially work on HomeOS projects with aligned incentives and transparent value attribution. Poor performers lose market access as their failures become visible in attestation streams.

Industry productivity improves for first time in decades. The 30-50% waste from misalignment gets eliminated. Buildings get delivered faster and cheaper while achieving better operational performance. Resources currently consumed by litigation and coordination overhead flow to actual value creation.

Built Environment Becomes Programmable

Smart Buildings also allow physical infrastructure to be managed as software. Buildings can gain new capabilities, operations can be optimized continuously, problems can be debugged systematically, and improvements can propagate automatically.

Infrastructure development then becomes product engineering. Traditional construction treats each building as a unique prototype requiring custom design, construction, and operation. Each Smart Building instead instantiates an evolving platform and inherits all improvements from previous instances.

Traditional infrastructure yields diminishing returns as buildings age and deteriorate. Programmable infrastructure yields increasing returns as network learning makes buildings smarter over time. Traditional infrastructure requires expensive periodic replacement, while programmable infrastructure undergoes continuous incremental improvement.

This extends beyond buildings to all physical infrastructure. Roads, bridges, utilities, public spaces—all become candidates for Smart Asset transformation once the pattern is validated for buildings. The built environment transitions from passive background to active economic participant.

Conclusion: Buildings as Programmable Institutions

Construction generates $12 trillion annually, and real estate comprises $280 trillion in value, while their coordination mechanisms remain those of the mid-twentieth century. Making buildings programmable addresses dysfunction across that market.

The proposal makes buildings Smart Assets: autonomous economic entities that carry their complete operational intelligence, enforce their rules, and execute their operations. Its six dependencies follow a specific order: shared semantic understanding, continuous provenance, collective learning, aligned incentives, transparent value attribution, and autonomous operation.

The calendar follows the validation sequence. Controlled pilots in 2026 establish that the foundations work operationally, and expansion to 1,000 buildings through 2027 validates network effects. Manufacturing scale from 2028 onward produces cost advantages through standardization. Each phase depends on the preceding validation, managing risk before further expansion.

Momentum's Mass Protocol infrastructure supplies the institutional layer through five programmable primitives: entities, ownership, financial instruments, identity, and consent. They enable buildings to operate as autonomous economic actors across jurisdictions.
