068 h2: Programmable ownership for land, housing, and community infrastructure
069 p: June 2025
070 h3: Disclaimer
071 p: This document is for research and design discussion. It does not constitute an offer to sell securities, financial advice, or legal advice. Real-world deployments require jurisdiction-specific legal structuring, licensing, and compliance.
076 h2: 1) The thesis
077 p: The built environment is the largest asset class humans maintain. Its ownership systems impose high transaction costs, obscure information, favor rent extraction, and remain politically fragile.
078 p: Archai is the protocol that turns land and buildings into programmable institutions with on-chain and off-chain components and access to arbitration, where:
079 li: people can enter through staking or buying,
080 li: earn ownership over time and contribution,
081 li: surplus is allocated by explicit constitutional logic, and
082 li: the whole system stays compliant, auditable, and jurisdiction-portable through Mass primitives and attestation streams.
083 p: Archai provides capital and governance infrastructure for many settlements and many civilizational models (Amagi and beyond).
084 h3: Why now: enabling infrastructure
085 p: Archai builds on existing Momentum systems:
086 li: Mass makes entities/ownership/consent programmable and enforceable through compliant APIs (legal infrastructure).
087 li: HomeOS makes buildings legible and continuously measurable (operational infrastructure), and that data directly improves valuation and risk models for tokenized assets.
088 li: Programmable legal stacks / SEZ modules make jurisdictional portability possible (jurisdictional infrastructure), with Smart Assets carrying compliance and producing attestation streams regulators can verify programmatically.
089 p: Together, these systems support the legal rights, operating records, and compliance checks that programmable property institutions require.
091 h2: 2) The problem
092 h3: 2.1 Ownership in real estate is structurally exclusionary
093 p: For most people, ownership requires a mortgage, a large down payment, creditworthiness, and legal expertise. Selling is slow and expensive, with high transaction costs.
094 h3: 2.2 Housing markets are dominated by rentier dynamics
095 p: The default incentives reward:
096 li: holding scarce assets,
097 li: restricting supply,
098 li: harvesting rent,
099 li: and capturing appreciation driven by policy and scarcity.
100 p: Abundant, well-maintained housing that serves its community requires different incentives.
101 h3: 2.3 Buildings are opaque, so risk is mispriced
102 p: Traditional real estate has persistent information asymmetries. HomeOS lets buildings report occupancy, energy, maintenance, and revenue streams continuously, providing the operational evidence that tokenization requires.
103 h3: 2.4 The coordination problem: land + community + infrastructure
104 p: Land ownership also encodes:
105 li: governance,
106 li: cultural norms,
107 li: maintenance commitments,
108 li: collective investment decisions.
109 p: Today, these are handled by slow legal processes and weak social trust. Archai’s premise is that they can be expressed as programmable institutional logic.
111 h2: 3) Required design principles
112 p: Archai is institutional infrastructure and must meet the legal, financial, and governance requirements below.
113 h3: 3.1 Legal reality first
114 p: A token requires enforceable rights to represent a secured asset. Archai vault assets are always paired with a real legal wrapper (SPV, trust, foundation, coop) that holds title and signs a protocol-aligned charter. The design must make these enforceable rights explicit from the outset.
115 h3: 3.2 Yield first, not appreciation first
116 p: We treat built assets as sources of cash flow (housing, services, utilities, data, commerce). Appreciation is not the core promise.
117 h3: 3.3 Anti-speculation must be engineered
118 p: “Non-speculative” is a set of constraints:
119 li: pricing rule,
120 li: transfer constraints,
121 li: premium capture rules,
122 li: exit windows and gates,
123 li: and governance limits.
124 h3: 3.4 Composability at the primitive level
125 p: Mass provides primitives for entities, ownership, identity, consent, and related functions. Archai composes them into institutions that own and operate built assets. In Momentum’s architecture, Archai tokenizes HomeOS buildings through native integration with these primitives.
126 h3: 3.5 Continuous attestations, not annual audits
127 p: The system needs evidence for compliance checks. Under the SEZ Legal Stack design, Smart Assets should generate cryptographically secured, legally enforceable attestation streams. These let programs verify compliance without exposing private data.
128 h3: 3.6 Progressive decentralization with explicit safeguards
129 p: Governance starts with safety: trustees, emergency powers, upgrade controls. It decentralizes only as mechanisms prove stable.
131 h2: 4) System model
132 h3: 4.1 Archai is a network of “Vault Institutions”
133 p: Each Archai project is a Vault:
134 li: a set of on-chain contracts,
135 li: bound to an off-chain legal wrapper,
136 li: holding a specific portfolio of land/buildings/infrastructure,
137 li: issuing stake positions and ownership claims,
138 li: distributing surplus according to encoded policy.
139 h3: 4.2 Archai separates “use” from “ownership”
140 p: Most housing systems bundle:
141 li: right to occupy (use),
142 li: financial exposure (ownership),
143 li: and governance power.
144 p: Archai treats them as separable primitives:
145 li: Use Rights: the ability to live/work in a unit under certain terms.
146 li: Stake Positions: time-locked capital with defined redemption and yield.
147 li: Ownership Units: equity-like claims on the underlying entity.
148 li: Contribution Credits: earned equity via labor/service, verified by attestations.
149 li: Governance Power: parameterized, scoped, and constrained.
150 p: Separating these lets us engineer “non-speculative” systems while still raising capital.
151 h3: 4.3 Archai is a policy engine for many civilizational models
152 p: A “civilizational model” combines policy parameters with a governance constitution:
153 li: rent policy,
154 li: surplus split,
155 li: equity issuance schedule,
156 li: contribution valuation rules,
157 li: secondary transfer constraints,
158 li: commons obligations.
159 p: Archai hosts multiple models. Each vault selects one and can migrate through governance.
161 h2: 5) Protocol architecture
162 h3: 5.1 Layer 1: Legal wrapper (Mass-native entity)
163 p: Each Vault corresponds to a legal entity (or entity set):
164 li: Asset SPV / Trust holds title and bank accounts.
165 li: Operator entity contracts services (maintenance, property management, construction).
166 li: Archai Foundation / protocol entity holds IP, administers upgrades, manages network treasury.
167 p: Mass provides software to form and maintain these entities automatically. It encodes their governance, ownership tables, identity checks, and consent workflows.
168 p: The legal wrapper operates as a programmable Smart Asset.
169 h3: 5.2 Layer 2: Vault contracts (capital + rights engine)
170 p: Vault contracts manage:
171 li: deposits,
172 li: stake terms,
173 li: redemptions,
174 li: distribution,
175 li: issuance of ownership units,
176 li: rent discount rules,
177 li: secondary transfer rules,
178 li: reserve accounts,
179 li: liquidation/wind-down logic.
180 h3: 5.3 Layer 3: Governance module (polycentric)
181 p: Vault governance excludes a simple “one token, one vote” model. It is:
182 li: scoped,
183 li: role-based,
184 li: bicameral where necessary,
185 li: with trustee/guardian failsafes.
186 h3: 5.4 Layer 4: Commons + network treasury
187 p: A portion of surplus flows to the Archai Commons Treasury. The initial proposal allocates 5–10%.
188 p: This treasury funds:
189 li: seeding new vaults,
190 li: resident subsidies,
191 li: protocol R&D,
192 li: and risk underwriting (insurance).
193 h3: 5.5 Layer 5: Evidence layer (HomeOS + attestation streams)
194 p: Buildings running HomeOS produce continuous operational data:
195 li: occupancy,
196 li: energy,
197 li: maintenance costs,
198 li: revenue streams.
199 p: This reduces information asymmetry and feeds valuation and risk models for tokenized assets.
200 p: The SEZ Legal Stack design uses attestation streams to prove required checks (KYC tier, AML screening, transfer restrictions) without revealing underlying private data.
201 h3: 5.6 Layer 6: Arbitration and dispute resolution
202 p: Every vault must have:
203 li: a defined dispute path,
204 li: arbitration clause,
205 li: on-chain evidence admissibility rules,
206 li: and emergency intervention rights.
207 p: Momentum’s ecosystem includes an ADGM-based arbitration court for digital asset and machine-intelligence workflows. Archai vaults should route disputes into such specialized venues by default.

210 h2: 6) Vault economics: stake-to-live, stake-to-own, and earned equity
211 p: The contracts must specify what participants receive for locking capital and how the vault produces returns without speculation.
212 h3: 6.1 Vault balance sheet and capital structure
213 p: Each vault combines private equity and real estate finance through the following capital structure:
214 li: Senior capital (bank debt / bonds)
Lowest risk, capped return, first claim.
215 li: Resident stake (time-locked deposits)
Medium risk, compensated via rent discounts + earned equity.
216 li: Programmatic equity (vault ownership units)
Variable return, receives residual surplus and some appreciation per policy.
217 li: Sponsor/operator equity
Paid for execution, absorbs early risk, earns if the asset performs.
218 li: Commons stake (network)
Receives protocol fee and may provide insurance backstops.
219 p: Solvency and redemptions depend on an explicit model of this capital structure.
220 h3: 6.2 Two primary stake products
221 h4: A) Resident Stake (stake-to-live)
222 p: Residents stake capital for a term. The initial proposal suggested 3–7 years.

They receive:
223 li: a rent discount (economic benefit now),
224 li: earned equity over time (wealth building),
225 li: governance participation (scoped),
226 li: and the right to redeem principal at maturity (subject to safety gates).
227 p: Resident Stake is a capitalized membership in a housing institution.
228 h4: B) Capital Stake (stake-to-build / stake-to-yield)
229 p: Non-resident capital providers stake into the vault with:
230 li: defined term,
231 li: defined distribution rules (coupon-like),
232 li: potentially equity kicker,
233 li: no occupancy rights.
234 p: Capital Stake finances additional housing supply while constraining speculation.
235 h3: 6.3 Earned equity
236 p: Earned equity rewards time, risk, and contribution instead of mere wealth.
237 p: The initial formula makes equity proportional to stake * multiplier / valuation. The model below adds explicit terms for time, risk, and contribution:

247 p: Where:
248 li: 𝑓(𝑇)f(T) is convex in time (long-term alignment rewarded),
249 li: 𝑅𝑖Ri​ is bounded (no infinite multipliers),
250 li: 𝐶𝑖(𝑡)Ci​(t) is capped per epoch to prevent gaming.
251 p: Equity accrues continuously or in epochs, minted as Vault Ownership Units.
252 h3: 6.4 Rent: discount as “coupon”
253 p: We formalize:
254 li: Let Base Rent be computed from:
255 li: operating costs,
256 li: reserves,
257 li: debt service,
258 li: targeted maintenance SLA,
259 li: and affordability policy.
260 li: A Resident Stake provides a discount that functions like the “interest payment” on their locked capital.
261 p: The vault borrows from residents and compensates them through reduced rent and earned equity.
262 h3: 6.5 Surplus allocation
263 p: Define:
264 p: 𝑆𝑢𝑟𝑝𝑙𝑢𝑠=𝑁𝑂𝐼−𝐷𝑒𝑏𝑡𝑆𝑒𝑟𝑣𝑖𝑐𝑒−𝑅𝑒𝑠𝑒𝑟𝑣𝑒𝑠Surplus=NOI−DebtService−Reserves
265 p: Then allocate surplus by policy:
266 li: Resident / staker distributions: 50–70% (initial proposal)
267 li: Reinvestment: 20–40% (initial proposal)
268 li: Protocol fee to Commons: 5–10% (initial proposal)
269 p: The allocation also includes:
270 li: Insurance / contingency buffer (can be part of reserves or funded via protocol treasury)
271 p: Surplus allocation defines the vault’s economic constitution.
273 h2: 7) Liquidity and exit without rentier speculation
274 p: Participants also need defined ways to exit. Most tokenized real estate fails to provide liquidity while constraining rentier speculation.
275 h3: 7.1 Three exit paths (each with constraints)
276 li: Redemption at maturity (primary)
277 li: Stake positions redeem at maturity at a deterministic rule:
278 li: principal less fees,
279 li: subject to solvency and redemption queues.
280 li: Secondary transfer of stake rights (bounded)

We formalize:
281 li: transfers only to whitelisted (KYC/KYB) recipients,
282 li: premium cap relative to NAV,
283 li: any premium above cap is routed to Commons (anti-speculation capture),
284 li: mandatory disclosure of vault state.
285 li: Cross-vault portability (network feature)
As Archai evolves into a registry, positions can be portable:
286 li: stake exits one vault and enters another under rules,
287 li: enabling mobility without liquidation cascades.
288 h3: 7.2 Redemption gates: preventing bank runs
289 p: Real assets are illiquid, so Archai cannot promise instant liquidity. It uses explicit redemption gates:
290 li: max redemption per epoch,
291 li: variable queue times tied to liquidity,
292 li: emergency halts when reserves breach threshold.
293 p: These gates protect solvency.
294 h3: 7.3 “Non-speculative” as a pricing rule
295 p: A vault can choose among policy modes:
296 li: NAV-bounded mode: ownership units trade within a tight band around NAV. Excess premium is taxed away.
297 li: Affordability-first mode: occupancy rights are non-transferable or transferable only at cost.
298 li: Growth mode (allowed but explicit): partial appreciation can accrue, but with caps and commons capture.
299 p: Speculation requires a deliberate policy choice and is disabled by default.
301 h2: 8) Governance and control surfaces
302 p: The initial proposal describes governance as “stake-based, resident-weighted, trustee-checked.” The roles below assign control over local rules, capital, and compliance, subject to constitutional limits.
303 h3: 8.1 Governance must be polycentric
304 p: Different decisions require different stakeholders.
305 p: Recommended structure:
306 h4: Resident Council
307 p: Controls:
308 li: community rules,
309 li: local service priorities,
310 li: cultural programming,
311 li: some budget allocations.
312 p: Voting weight: occupancy + contribution (not raw capital).
313 h4: Capital Council
314 p: Controls:
315 li: leverage limits,
316 li: reserve policy,
317 li: large capex,
318 li: refinancing terms,
319 li: issuance policy.
320 p: Voting weight: stake and ownership units (bounded).
321 h4: Trustee/Guardian
322 p: Controls:
323 li: legal compliance,
324 li: emergency actions,
325 li: fraud response,
326 li: enforcing constitutional constraints.
327 p: Trustees connect vault governance to its legal obligations.
328 h3: 8.2 Constitutional constraints (hard-coded)
329 p: Certain parameters must be upgrade-resistant:
330 li: maximum leverage,
331 li: minimum reserves,
332 li: affordability floors,
333 li: anti-speculation constraints,
334 li: disclosure requirements.
335 p: Governance cannot authorize treasury theft or leverage beyond the constitutional limit.
336 h3: 8.3 Evidence-based governance
337 p: Governance proposals must reference:
338 li: attested operating metrics (HomeOS),
339 li: audited financials,
340 li: risk reports,
341 li: and predicted impacts.
342 p: These records connect governance decisions to the building’s operations, finances, and risks.

417 h3: 11.2 Distribution principles
418 li: Incentivize early vault bootstrapping and limit the duration of subsidies.
419 li: Reward verified contribution (builders, maintainers, operators) more than passive speculation.
420 li: Fund long-term commons (treasury).
421 li: Avoid short-term emission schedules that create mercenary behavior.
422 h3: 11.3 Regulatory posture
423 p: Vault assets are likely regulated financial instruments in many jurisdictions. Mass-native compliance must constrain distributions and transfers accordingly.
424 p: $ARCH may be regulated if it carries explicit cash-flow claims. Governance and underwriting collateral with carefully scoped economics may offer a clearer regulatory position. The treatment depends on jurisdiction, so Archai must support distinct legal modules.
