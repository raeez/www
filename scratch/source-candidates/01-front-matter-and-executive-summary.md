## Executive Summary

Special economic zones have shaped economic development over the past sixty-five years. From Shannon Airport's experiment in 1959 to the more than 5,000 zones counted in the United Nations' last global census, small territories with different rules have become metropolises and reorganized hundreds of millions of lives. China's reform era, which the World Bank credits with history's largest reduction in poverty, began with four bounded experiments on the southern coast. The zone is among the modern era's most consequential institutional inventions.

Yet the zones that succeeded — Shannon, Singapore, Shenzhen, Dubai — remained exceptions. Geography, statecraft, and timing gave them advantages other places could not copy. Most of the thousands that followed failed as isolated enclaves: each rebuilt its administration, learned nothing from other zones, and confined its companies and institutional knowledge to one jurisdiction. A company with a decade of verified operation in one zone had to prove itself again in the next. Part I examines that pattern through five chapters on what successful zones did, what failed zones lacked, and the limits both encountered.

The rest of the book examines how zones can connect. Artificial intelligence can now execute the regulated work of a company: formation, identity, compliance, treasury, tax, governance, and audit. An operating system built for this work can run companies and a jurisdiction's regulated economy. A government can deploy it in under 90 days for under $10 million, a fraction of a traditional industrial park's cost and construction time. Two such jurisdictions can then negotiate a **corridor**: a bilateral agreement specifying which of each other's verifications they recognize, and at what grade. A company's verified standing can cross that corridor while money and assets move through each jurisdiction's lawful channels. This transfer of standing connects experiments that the zone movement has until now conducted in isolation.

The historical argument is that successful zones approximated three conditions a network can supply by design: competitive pressure, institutional learning, and economic activity from the first day. The architectural argument is that these conditions can now be built. Mass, the AI-native operating system described here, runs the regulated life of companies and jurisdictions across more than thirteen jurisdictions, carrying more than ten thousand companies. Momentum builds and operates zones on it, launching each with operating businesses to overcome the emptiness that defeats most new zones. The proposed research laboratory would turn operating data into the first systematic experimental record of zone policy. The book introduces its design in Chapter 9 before examining the operating evidence in Part III.

The political argument concerns sovereign choice. Each jurisdiction owns and operates its deployment, chooses its corridors, and grants recognition at a specified grade with the right to revoke it. The design rests on the proposition that governments compete best as service providers when participation is itself a sovereign act.

This book addresses ministers weighing zone proposals, allocators assessing the asset class, builders choosing where to work, and citizens whose governments' development promises have outrun results. It distinguishes systems in production, signed commitments awaiting construction, and designs still to be built. The technology is in production, the first sovereign deployments operate, and further deployments are in motion. The network can extend what individual zones achieved to the world economy. Whether it does so depends on how, and by whom, it is built.
