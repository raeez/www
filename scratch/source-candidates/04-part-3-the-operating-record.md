## Chapter 10: The Operating Record

### The Verb Matches the Evidence

The architecture in Part II makes claims about deployment, company operations, and network demand. This chapter compares those claims with the operating record. It distinguishes production operations from signed commitments awaiting construction and from early conversations. Early conversations remain outside the operating count.

### What Operates Today

Mass is in production today across **more than thirteen sovereign and commercial jurisdictions**, carrying **more than ten thousand companies** and roughly **ten billion dollars** of asset volume on the platform.

The footprint comprises the following stages and forms of operation. In the **United States**, commercial operation across seven states, behind a federal money-services registration. In **Honduras**, a live integration with Próspera, the special economic jurisdiction on Roatán: agents query the jurisdiction's live registry through its own e-Portal, run name checks, and stage entity actions under the Roatán common-law code — an autonomous system interacting with a sovereign registry. In **Hong Kong** and **Singapore**, commercial operation. In **Abu Dhabi (ADGM)** and **Dubai (DIFC)**, registered and operating presence in the two Gulf financial centres whose regulatory architecture Part I examined. Corridor relationships and additional sovereign deployments are in flight beyond these.

The list covers common-law islands, Gulf financial centres, an American multi-state footprint, and a Central American startup jurisdiction. This range tests Chapter 6's claim that the architecture can serve different legal traditions and stages of development. Its ten thousand companies constitute an operating population at the time of writing.

### What the Record Supports

The record bears on three claims from Part II.

**The deployment claim is an operating claim.** A sovereign node stands up in under 90 days for under $10 million — using the jurisdiction's existing legislation and registries, with no civil works on the critical path. Against Part I's benchmarks — industrial parks at $50 million to $500 million and half a decade to first tenant — the cost of establishing a zone has fallen by two orders of magnitude. The lower entry cost explains the change in who initiates these conversations.

**Small-registry economics are the proven case, and they are the model's natural early adopters.** Small fees across large populations of companies, renewing annually, are the economics that built the BVI, Cayman, and the Seychelles registries. A small jurisdiction can justify a node through its existing registry economics, operated at machine speed and connected to the network's demand.

**The network multiplier is already visible in a small network.** Every company on the substrate is one corridor away from every jurisdiction the network adds; every jurisdiction that joins is one agreement away from ten thousand companies. Each addition therefore extends the reach of existing participants. This is the accumulation of value that isolated zones in Part I lacked, and the record shows it beginning.

## Chapter 11: The Deployment Method

### Phase 0: Foundation

Deployment begins with the political and legal decisions that determine how the node will operate.

A stakeholder map identifies who supports the zone, who can block it, and whose non-objection it needs. Finance seeks revenue, commerce investment, labor jobs, security services control, and local authorities influence. Serving only one constituency creates opposition among the others. Part I showed how neglecting these interests, including those of permanent secretaries, defeated zone proposals.

The legal foundation is deliberately minimal: the smallest viable instrument set that designates the zone, names its governing authority, fixes its fiscal terms, and establishes dispute resolution. The instrument set must permit amendment. Many jurisdictions discover they already hold most of what they need — existing zone statutes, existing registries, existing licensing regimes — and the foundation phase becomes an inventory exercise; those jurisdictions already have the necessary legislation. Those are the under-90-day configurations.

Site selection follows demand. Part I showed the failures of zones conceived principally as real-estate projects. Deployment starts with the smallest site that serves the anchor tenants well and expands as demand justifies it.

### Phase 1: The Node Goes Live

The first technical task is configuration. The jurisdiction's rules — its formation requirements, licence categories, fee schedules, disclosure regimes — are encoded into the rule layer. This encoding exposes ambiguities, contradictions, and obsolete provisions, because precise execution requires a precise rule. More than one government has found the configuration exercise to be the most rigorous review its regulations ever received.

The first entities need to tolerate early defects and provide useful feedback. Momentum's corporate-services provider, banking relationships, payments capability, and workspace begin operating alongside them. Incorporations, accounts, transactions, and payroll in the first month demonstrate the systems described in Chapter 8.

Early problems guide revision. Deployment proceeds with measurement through the attestation stream and weekly corrections; waiting for perfection prevented other zones from proceeding.

### Phases 2 and 3: Growth and Maturity

As the zone grows, operators establish consistent procedures for work the pioneers improvised. The portfolio shifts from push to pull as organic formations outnumber recruited ones. Specialization sharpens — a zone discovers what it is becoming known for, and resources follow the discovery, wherever the original brochure pointed. The first corridor negotiations open, typically with the partner whose complementarity is most obvious, and the zone's companies acquire their first second harbor.

At maturity, established procedures let the institution continue beyond its founders. Its innovations can inform national policy, as Shannon's tax architecture informed Ireland's and Shenzhen's labor contracts informed China's. The mature node also contributes corridor capacity, configuration patterns, and evidence for later participants to use.

The full cycle from foundation to maturity takes about three years. The under-90-day claim covers the node going live. These are different milestones: technical deployment precedes the surrounding economy's maturation.

## Chapter 12: The Measurement Framework

### Beyond Vanity Metrics

Registered companies are mostly shells, announced jobs mostly construction, and committed investment mostly land plays in the zone industry's headline statistics. The failed zones in Part I passed through periods when those numbers still looked healthy. Measuring success therefore requires evidence beyond activity alone.

The framework runs on a hierarchy. **Activity** — formations, transactions, filings — proves something is happening. **Output** — operating companies, filled jobs, recurring revenue — proves the activity is real. **Outcome** — falling compliance costs, shrinking processing times, rising firm survival — proves the reality creates value. **Impact** — rising incomes, deepening skills, local firms winning beyond the zone — proves the value reaches people. The lower tiers can be gamed; the upper tiers cannot, and the framework weights them accordingly. A zone is succeeding when the impact tier moves, and merely advertising when only the activity tier does.

### Measurement as a By-Product of Operation

Traditional zone statistics come from expensive annual surveys, with delays and self-reporting bias. Attestations record formation times, compliance costs, and transaction volumes during the operations themselves. These continuous, complete, verifiable records resist the optimistic reclassification common in self-reported development statistics.

Access rules protect the privacy of companies and citizens. The system gives each authorized reader exactly what its rules permit, and proves compliance facts without disclosing underlying records where those rules allow. It logs every official access so that supervisory use is itself auditable. Chapter 14 examines the risk of abuse at this boundary.

### Transparency as Competitive Position

A zone publishes its aggregate formation times, service levels, and growth continuously in a form anyone can verify. Investors can price that performance, companies can compare jurisdictions, and researchers can study their results. The government receives current evidence for managing its economy.

Across the network, the published record becomes comparative knowledge of a kind the zone movement never accumulated: which policies move which metrics in which contexts, visible across dozens of jurisdictions running comparable measurement. Chapter 9's laboratory turns that record into science. Zones compete on performance while sharing the knowledge that improves it. This separates the competition for companies from the secrecy and beggar-thy-neighbor policies documented in Part I.
