## Which probes can couple to a boundary? {#opening-consistent-couplings}

On a circle, one quantum of the second harmonic carries the same energy as two quanta of the first harmonic of a free current. Prepare the normalized sum of those two occupations, or their normalized difference. Count the quanta in either harmonic. The two preparations give the same probabilities. Now interchange the two occupations. The sum is unchanged and the difference changes sign, so the exchange has a definite reading, \(+1\) on the sum and \(-1\) on the difference. Which operation on the boundary field could measure that sign? [Two preparations and their joint measurement](#ch01-two-preparations)

Let \(T\) be a Hermitian observable on the span of the two unit occupations \(a,b\). An instrument calibrated to \(T\) has mean \(m_\pm=\langle\pm|T|\pm\rangle\) in the states \(\lvert\pm\rangle=(a\pm b)/\sqrt2\). Write \(T_{ab}=\langle a|T|b\rangle\). Expanding the two expectations gives
\[
 m_+-m_-=T_{ab}+T_{ba}.
\]
A counter of occupations has both matrix entries zero. For the exchange both are one. The choice of interaction with the probe determines which distinction the experiment can make. Which interactions can a boundary admit, and what happens to them when two probes are joined?

Take a finite-dimensional Lie algebra \(\mathfrak g\) over a field \(k\) of characteristic zero. Its infinitesimal symmetries have a basis \(x_a\) and brackets \([x_a,x_b]=\sum_c f_{ab}{}^c x_c\). The probe supplies operators \(T_a\) in a unital algebra \(D\) concentrated in degree zero. Introduce an anticommuting variable \(c^a\) of degree one for each \(x_a\). On their exterior algebra put the differential \(dc^c=-\tfrac12\sum_{a,b} f_{ab}{}^c c^a c^b\), extending by the graded product rule. Its square vanishes by Jacobi. A proposed coupling is the degree-one element \(\alpha=\sum_a c^a\otimes T_a\). Its differential and two successive insertions give
\[
\begin{aligned}
 d\alpha+\alpha^2
 &=\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}c^ac^b\otimes K_{ab},\\
 K_{ab}&=[T_a,T_b]-\sum_c f_{ab}{}^cT_c .
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the connection \(d+\alpha\), acting by left multiplication on coefficients, squares to zero precisely when the probe operators realise the Lie brackets. The quotient of the free associative algebra on the \(x_a\) by these very relations is \(U(\mathfrak g)\), the universal enveloping algebra. Choosing a unital map \(U(\mathfrak g)\to D\) chooses one consistent coupling, even when the target operators do not commute. [The coupling equation and its universal algebra](#ch02-consistent-lie-couplings)

For the Heisenberg algebra take \([x,y]=z\) with \(z\) central. On the polynomial probe \(k[q]\) let \(T_x\) differentiate and \(T_y\) multiply by \(q\). Acting on \(q^n\), their commutator gives \(((n+1)-n)q^n=q^n\). The coefficient of \(c^xc^y\) in the coupling equation forces \(T_z=1\). No nonzero finite-dimensional probe over \(k\) has that central action, since the trace of a matrix commutator is zero and the trace of its identity is its dimension. A universal algebra can describe both the probe that exists and the obstruction to a smaller one.

A graded probe can also supply a homotopy for a relation and further homotopies among these choices. In the augmented bar construction a cut of a word gives the two inputs for a product in the probe algebra. If \(C\) is a coaugmented conilpotent differential graded coalgebra and \(D\) an augmented differential graded algebra, a twisting cochain is a degree-one map \(\tau:C\to D\) that vanishes on the coaugmentation and takes values in the augmentation ideal. With the cohomological convention its equation is \(d_{\rm Hom}\tau+\tau\star\tau=0\), where \(\star\) cuts by the reduced coproduct and multiplies the two outputs with the tensor signs. Specifying \(\tau\) is equivalent to specifying an augmented differential graded algebra map from the cobar algebra \(\Omega C\) to \(D\). The cobar generators record the values of \(\tau\), and preservation of the differential is exactly its equation. Which dual represents a physical line coupling depends on the fields, allowed couplings and completion for that line. [A twisting cochain and its two-letter test](#ch05-twisting-cochain-probe) · [Paquette and Williams, §§5.1–5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.10257v4#S5.SS2)

## Acting on two probes and exchanging them {#opening-composition-of-probes}

On one probe the product \(xy\) acts by applying \(y\) and then \(x\). To act on two probes with the same infinitesimal symmetry we must say how \(x\) acts on their tensor product. If \(V,W\) carry actions \(T_x^V,T_x^W\), the simultaneous action is \(T_x^V\otimes1+1\otimes T_x^W\). The two summands on different factors commute, so taking brackets gives \(T_{[x,y]}^V\otimes1+1\otimes T_{[x,y]}^W\). By the universal property, the formula extends to an algebra map from \(U(\mathfrak g)\) into its tensor square. This map is the coproduct
\[
 \Delta(x)=x\otimes1+1\otimes x .
\]
For example, the matrix \(E_{12}\) takes \(e_2\) to \(e_1\) and kills \(e_1\). On \(e_2\otimes e_2\) it produces \(e_1\otimes e_2+e_2\otimes e_1\). On three probes either bracketing places the same infinitesimal operator once in each of the three factors.

What more can we learn by exchanging two probes? Take the fundamental two-dimensional evaluation modules of the rational Yangian for \(\mathfrak{gl}_2\), labelled by \(a,b\). Write \(u=a-b\), fix a nonzero deformation parameter \(\lambda\), and use the common basis to write \(P(e_i\otimes e_j)=e_j\otimes e_i\). For \(u\ne\pm\lambda\) the following normalised matrix intertwines the ordered and opposite actions. After the flip the target is \(V_b\otimes V_a\), with the probe at \(b\) first.
\[
 R(u)=\frac{uI+\lambda P}{u+\lambda},\qquad c_{a,b}=\tau R(u).
\]
At \(u=2\lambda\), applying \(c_{a,b}\) to \(e_1^a\otimes e_2^b\) gives \((2e_2^b\otimes e_1^a+e_1^b\otimes e_2^a)/3\). The symmetric channel fixes the scalar normalisation of \(R\) by requiring its eigenvalue to be one. Here \(u\) separates the evaluation parameters of the modules. In the regular translation construction the state fields of this algebra are polynomial in their insertion positions. In four-dimensional Chern–Simons theory a separate perturbative calculation of Wilson-line crossings supplies the dependence on the holomorphic separation. [The marked intertwiner](#ch12-combined-probe-exchange) · [Costello, Witten and Yamazaki, §§2 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.09993)

Three crossings can be performed in two orders. Equality of the two composites is the braid relation, or the Yang–Baxter equation before the final flips. Exchanging one probe with a pair that has already been joined asks a further question. For example, replace the flip on every pair of vector spaces by \(2\tau\). Both three-crossing paths acquire the factor \(2^3\). But exchanging once past a tensor product gives the factor \(2\), whereas exchanging past its two factors successively gives \(4\). The hexagon tests exactly this compatibility. With four probes a pentagon tests the consistency of the changes of bracketing themselves. The structure of a chiral quantum group must also specify the products of fields at varying positions on the domain where these comparisons can be made. [The field hexagon and the coproduct](#opening-spectral-symmetry)

## Which interaction a boundary detects {#opening-boundary-interactions}

Composing probe operations can reveal a coefficient of the bulk action itself. In one variable the first comparison already fits in a two-dimensional space of boundary cohomology classes.

Can two interactions have the same binary product on a boundary? In a B-twisted Landau–Ginzburg model a function \(W\), the potential, specifies the bulk interaction. Work over \(k[[x]]\), with \(k\) a field of characteristic zero. Take one even and one odd basis vector at the boundary. For \(W=x^d,\ d\ge3\), put
\[
Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\x^{d-1}&0\end{pmatrix} .
\]
\[
\psi=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-x^{d-2}&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The first matrix squares to \(W\,1\), a factorization of the potential. The differential on a boundary operator is its graded commutator with \(Q\). The odd field \(\psi\) is closed, while the matrix with a single \(1\) in its lower-left entry has differential \(x\,1\). Since \(\psi^2=-x^{d-2}1\), its square is zero in cohomology for every \(d\ge3\). What sequence of boundary insertions could distinguish \(d=3\) from \(d=4\)?

Perturb the upper-right entry to \(x+t\). Choosing the lower-left entry to be \((W(x)-W(-t))/(x+t)\) makes the new square \((W(x)-W(-t))1\). Relative to the fixed bulk square, the scalar left over is \(t^3\) for \(W=x^3\) and \(-t^4\) for \(W=x^4\). In the given coordinate, successive insertions of \(\psi\) recover these coefficients as higher products with scalar output. Projecting the outputs modulo the scalar unit erases these products. A parameter is flat when the scalar error vanishes there. Over \(k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^4)\) the quartic admits \(t=\epsilon\), while the cubic leaves \(\epsilon^3\ne0\). Doubling the cubic preserves the set of flat parameters and doubles its error at each \(t\). For \(t=F(s)\), with \(F(0)=0\) and \(F'(0)\ne0\), the same bulk potential \(W(x)\) gives the scalar series \(-W(-F(s))\). [Flat couplings of the two-state boundary](#ch19-flat-boundary-couplings) · [Reading the potential from higher products](#ch19-boundary-potential)

For the cubic the divided difference is \(x^2-xt+t^2\), since \((x+t)(x^2-xt+t^2)=x^3+t^3\). For the quartic it is \(x^3-x^2t+xt^2-t^3\), whose product with \(x+t\) is \(x^4-t^4\). More generally fix the bulk coordinate \(x\), the based boundary parameter \(t\), and \(W\in x^3 k[[x]]\). In the general factorisation the lower-left entry is \(W(x)/x\). Its displaced matrix has tangent \(\psi=E_{12}-(W(x)/x^2)E_{21}\) at \(t=0\), where \(E_{ij}\) is the matrix unit in row \(i\), column \(j\). Write \([x^n]W\) for the coefficient of \(x^n\) in the potential. In the strictly unital minimal model with the convention \(d\alpha+\alpha^2\), the coefficient of \(t^n\) in \(-W(-t)\) gives
\[
 m_n(\psi,\ldots,\psi)=(-1)^{n+1}[x^n]W\,\mathbf1 .
\]
Changing \(t\) to \(2s\) changes the leading cubic coefficient by a factor of eight and leaves its order three. The coefficient belongs to the marked coordinates. In a bar construction that omits the unit from its words, the scalar component on an \(n\)-letter word can be retained as a curvature functional. Here the boundary algebra before deformation has zero nullary product. The cubic coefficient comes from three insertions. [The minimal model and a change of coordinate](#ch19-boundary-potential) · [Dyckerhoff, Theorem 5.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0904.4713v5)

There are two different tests of the class of \(x\) for the cubic. Let \(h=E_{21}\) and \(\psi=E_{12}-xE_{21}\). Then \(Qh+hQ=xI\), so \(h\) contracts multiplication by \(x\) on this one boundary. Yet \(\psi h+h\psi=I\). The chosen contraction leaves a unit when it is passed across the odd operation. On the identity interface, which must transmit all the boundary maps coherently, the diagonal equations give \(k[[x]]/(3x^2)\). Thus \(x\) does survive in the derived centre of the supported two-periodic category at this isolated critical point. A generating boundary equipped with its full differential graded endomorphism algebra can compute this centre. [The diagonal calculation](#ch19-diagonal-and-trace) · [Dyckerhoff, Corollary 6.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0904.4713v5#page=36)

## What an interface must carry {#opening-operation-kernels}

Passing a probe through an interface transports its operations as well as its states. To decide whether two probes can be joined before or after crossing it, we need to know what joining does to *maps* of either probe. Suppose a sector is generated under colimits by the regular right module of a differential graded \(k\)-algebra \(A\), over a field. Let \(\mu\) be a binary \(k\)-linear operation on its derived category of right modules, with the coherent action on mapping complexes and preservation of colimits in each variable. Its value \(P=\mu(A,A)\) is a right \(A\)-module. Left multiplication on the first or second copy of \(A\) induces two graded-commuting input actions on \(P\). With these actions the operation is
\[
 \mu(M,N)\simeq
 (M\otimes_k N)\otimes^{\mathbf L}_{A\otimes_k A}P .
\]
Indeed a free resolution of each input builds the operation out of copies of \(P\). Its differentials apply exactly those input actions. To join this output to a third probe, tensor the kernel’s right action over \(A\) with the corresponding left action of the next kernel. [The operation-kernel proposition and proof](#ch17-proposition-operation-kernels) · [Toën, Corollary 7.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0408337#page=40)

Take \(A=k\times k\) with projections \(e_1=(1,0)\) and \(e_2=(0,1)\). A right module \(M\) splits as \(Me_1\oplus Me_2\). Consider two rules for joining \(M,N\). The first outputs \((Me_1)\otimes_k(Ne_1)\), the second \((Me_2)\otimes_k(Ne_1)\), both placed in the first output sector. Each sends \((A,A)\) to the same one-dimensional right module. On the first input the endomorphism \(a\mapsto e_1a\) acts on that output as the identity for the first rule and as zero for the second. Consequently the outputs on \((e_1A,A)\) are \(k\) and \(0\). This is the information the left actions carry.

Recovering the operation by this formula establishes a round trip on the sector the probes generate. To identify a bulk interaction one must also start from a bulk theory and calculate its action across the interface. For the free current this can be done by a contraction with the independently prepared field. For the stable protected M2 algebra the comparison uses the formal deformation parameters of the twisted theory. Identifying an absolute amplitude on a cut requires the preparations on the two sides and the pairing between them. [A bulk contraction on an occupied disk](#ch07-supported-bulk-action) · [The first protected membrane commutator](#ch14-m2-first-jet-commutator) · [Gluing two independently specified caps](#ch18-example-cutting-a-two-label-surface)

### What a simpler observation forgets {#opening-forgetting-operations}

Each comparison has an operation that can lose the answer. Keeping only binary multiplication sends both the cubic and the quartic boundary products to the same exterior algebra. At two marked points, restriction to distinct positions kills a complex supported on the diagonal. Let \(X\) be the complex line and \(i\) the inclusion of its origin. The constant sheaf \(k_X\) and \(k_X\oplus i_*k\) agree on the punctured line. At the origin the second has one more stalk dimension. In the two-sector kernel the loss occurs when one forgets what the input idempotent does. In a graded family of fields even the tensor product can impose a restriction. If \(e_0,e_1,\ldots\) are linearly independent in \(W\), the series \(\sum_{n\ge0}e_nz^n\) belongs to \(W[[z]]\), hence to \(W((z))\). An element of \(k((z))\otimes_k W\) uses only finitely many vectors of \(W\), so it cannot give that series. [Extension across a collision](#ch04-normal-link) · [Laurent series and the spectator space](#ch08)

Closing an experiment loses different information. The matrices \(I\) and \(I+E_{12}\) on \(k^2\) have equal trace, while their actions on \(e_2\) differ by \(e_1\). Dividing by the vacuum amplitude loses an overall scalar instead. Two fillings with functionals \(Z\) and \(3Z\), where \(Z(1)\ne0\), give the same ratio \(Z(O)/Z(1)\) for every insertion \(O\) and different amplitudes for the empty insertion. The open kernel, the state and the absolute amplitude answer distinct questions about the same preparation. [Keeping the ends open](#opening-open-ends) · [Measuring the vacuum amplitude](#opening-filling)

## What a contour measures {#opening-contours}

The Lie algebra example fixed the relations that an instrument must respect. On a curve one can move two insertions before coupling them to a third, or bring them to the same point. The contour weights in the free current let us determine exactly which operation survives each choice.

To turn one occupation into the other, we need a boundary operation that selects a harmonic. In a free abelian current theory, try an unweighted loop on the boundary surface around the point where a charged line ends. In a complex coordinate \(z\), a line of charge \(a\) at \(w\) produces a current with polar part \(a/(z-w)\). Integrating the current once counterclockwise and dividing by \(2\pi i\) gives \(a\). Put two lines of opposite charge inside the loop and the reading is zero. Moving one of them still changes the field. Which boundary measurements detect that change? [A charge measured by a contour](#ch03-charged-current-contour) · [The two neutral preparations](#ch11-neutral-ray-pair)

We can calibrate the loop by putting a second current at its centre in place of the charged endpoint. Suppose the two currents have a double pole of strength \(\kappa\), the level of the current. Its coefficient multiplies the vacuum field \(\mathbf1\), which acts as the identity. On a loop \(\gamma\) winding once counterclockwise about \(w\), the unweighted contour reads
\[
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{\kappa\mathbf1}{(z-w)^2}\,dz=0 .
\]
This reading is zero for every value of the level. To measure the strength of the pole, we must change the weight of the contour.

A contour on a surface can measure more than the charge it encloses. Let \(J(z)\,dz\) be a holomorphic current in a coordinate disk. Suppose that two insertions of this current have the singular product
\[
J(z)J(w)\sim\frac{\kappa\,\mathbf 1}{(z-w)^2}.
\]
Here \(\mathbf 1\) is the vacuum field, \(\kappa\) is the level, and the two sides have the same polar part at \(z=w\). Integrate around \(w\) with a holomorphic weight \(f\). The residue of \(f(z)J(z)J(w)\,dz\) is \(\kappa f'(w)\mathbf 1\). A constant weight gives zero. The weight \(f(z)=z-w\) measures \(\kappa\). Replacing the two fields by a value at coincidence would lose the quantity the contour measures. [The free current](#heisenberg-current)

Draw a second contour around the first. Integration in the two possible orders defines two operations on any state inserted at the centre of the disk. As one contour passes the other, their difference is the integral of the polar part at the crossing. In a coordinate centred at the insertion, put \(J_m=\operatorname{Res}_z z^mJ(z)\,dz\). The inner residue with weight \(z^m\) is \(\kappa m w^{m-1}\mathbf1\). The remaining residue with weight \(w^n\) consequently gives
\[
[J_m,J_n]=\kappa m\,\delta_{m+n,0}\,\mathrm{id}.
\]
Positive and negative modes of the same frequency can detect one another. A current acting on its vacuum, a contour enclosing two fields, and an operator on states are three uses of the same local product. Even in this example it matters on which side of an insertion a contour lies.

At level one these modes realize the exchange. Write \(p_j\) for creation of a quantum of harmonic \(j\). Normalize the vacuum to have norm one and take \(J_n\) adjoint to \(J_{-n}\). The two products \(J_1^2J_{-1}^2\) and \(J_2J_{-2}\) each return twice the vacuum. The orthogonal occupations \(p_1^2\) and \(p_2\) therefore both have squared norm two. Their unit representatives are \(a=p_1^2/\sqrt2\) and \(b=p_2/\sqrt2\). The mode \(J_2\) removes a quantum of the second harmonic, and two applications of \(J_{-1}\) add two quanta to the first. Adding this operation to its adjoint gives \(O=J_{-1}^2J_2+J_{-2}J_1^2\). The contour commutator gives \(J_j=j\partial_{p_j}\) on polynomials for \(j>0\). Thus \(Oa=2b\) and \(Ob=2a\). On these states \(O\) is twice the exchange. [The two paths through the vacuum](#fig-energy-two-exchange)

The exchange can be prepared from the interior as well. Put the disk on the boundary of a free abelian half-space and take the fluctuation scale \(\hbar\) and the bulk action coefficient \(\kappa_{\mathrm b}\) with \(\hbar\kappa_{\mathrm b}=1\), so that the boundary current has level one. A unit interior probe acts on the chosen boundary polynomials by \(\beta(u_j)=\partial_{p_j}\). To realize \(J_2\) in the first path requires a probe of strength two. The return path uses two unit first-harmonic probes at successive heights. The disk gives an independent comparison. Its vacuum harmonic amplitudes \(Z_1,Z_2\) are independent centred complex Gaussians with mean-square magnitudes \(1,2\). The sum and difference preparations weight that law by \(\left|(Z_1^2\pm Z_2)/2\right|^2\). In each sample record \(C=2\operatorname{Re}(Z_1^2\overline{Z_2})\). Its mean in the two preparations is \(+2\) or \(-2\), equal to the corresponding sharp outcome of \(O\). The disk reading itself fluctuates. [The probe contraction and the disk integral](#ch11-relative-phase)

What does the same disk sample give if we use all its harmonics? On a circle of radius \(0<r<1\) in the unit disk, multiply the squared gradient of the real harmonic field by its outward radial derivative. Let \(\Phi_r\) be \(r^3/8\) times the angular average of this product. The first two harmonics contribute \(r^4C/2\). Every other term has zero mean in both preparations. The full reading therefore has mean \(+r^4\) in the sum and \(-r^4\) in the difference. As the circle approaches the boundary, these means approach the two exchange eigenvalues. Under the vacuum law, the probability that \(\Phi_r\) lies in any fixed bounded interval tends to zero as \(r\uparrow1\). The same holds for each of the two prepared laws. [The cubic reading on a disk](#eq-cubic-disk-observable) · [Why a single reading leaves bounded intervals](#eq-cubic-disk-escape)

The two occupations have the same harmonic grade, two. A ring with quadratic single-particle kinetic energy supplies a Hamiltonian that splits their sum and difference. Put an odd number \(N\ge3\) of free spinless fermions of mass \(m\) on a ring of circumference \(L\), and excite density waves at the upper edge of the filled momenta. Raising the highest occupied momentum by one step costs \(\varepsilon_1=N(2\pi\hbar/L)^2/(2m)\). At added momentum \(2(2\pi\hbar/L)\), the two stationary states are the sum and the difference above. Their excitation energies are \(\varepsilon_1(2+2/N)\) and \(\varepsilon_1(2-2/N)\). Resolving a gap of \(4\varepsilon_1/N\) therefore distinguishes them in this free-ring model. For \(N=101\) fermions the gap is \(4\varepsilon_1/101\simeq 0.0396\,\varepsilon_1\). At higher excitation two different stationary states can have the same energy and different occupied momenta. [The kinetic-energy calculation](#ch11-proposition-kinetic-energy-in-the-density-waves) · [Two states with the same cubic reading](#fig-schur-readings)

Now put the disk in the boundary of a three-dimensional space. An interior insertion and a boundary experiment can be specified independently. Suppose the interior contains a line ending at the centre of the disk. What is its charge, how does it act on a second line, and what state does the filling of the disk prepare? A boundary description ought to predict each answer using operations available on the disk. The comparison has to hold also when two ends come together. Otherwise two successive calculations of the same measurement could give different answers according to when the ends were joined.

Fix a boundary condition and the sectors on which its fields act. First ask what becomes of a product of fields when a disk around them is cut into two smaller disks. For three insertions \(a,b,c\) in that order, a first cut can leave \(a,b\) together or leave \(b,c\) together. To reconstruct the original product we must compare those two choices. An interface poses a second question. If it carries a sector to a different boundary, can we recover the sector by passing it back? A filling poses a third. The free disk predicts the joint law of all positive harmonics from two preparations, one by charge lines and one by a harmonic source. Gluing a second filling onto that disk also uses the absolute vacuum amplitude, which normalization cancels. Each comparison requires its own operation on boundary data.

## What can be recovered by cutting {#opening-ordered-cuts}

For three fields in a fixed order there are two ways to make successive binary collisions. One brings \(a\) to \(b\) and their product to \(c\), the other brings \(b\) to \(c\) first. Each prescription starts near its own diagonal in the space of three marked points. On a domain where both can be compared, write their products as \((ab)c\) and \(a(bc)\). When the fields form a cochain complex with differential \(d\), the comparison itself can be an operation. For closed inputs a degree-minus-one operation \(H\) gives
\[
dH(a,b,c)=(ab)c-a(bc).
\]
Thus the products agree on cohomology. A fourth field can be multiplied with \(H(a,b,c)\), so the choice of \(H\) still enters the next calculation. The five parenthesizations of four fields form a pentagon. Its two paths must be related by a further homotopy. An \(A_\infty\) multiplication keeps these operations and their compatibility at every number of inputs.

A second way to bring the same points together explains why a collision can remember more than a commutator. Write \(t=z-w\). For a product \(\sum_n c_n(w)t^n\), the residue with multiplier \(t^{-q-1}\) gives \(c_q(w)\), even for \(q\ge0\). Thus a pole in the *test* can recover the regular part of the product. Beilinson and Drinfeld allow precisely such Laurent tests for fields on a smooth curve \(X\). Let \(j\) include the distinct pairs into \(X^2\), and let \(\Delta:X\to X^2\) include the diagonal. For a right differential module \(A\) of fields, the geometric chiral operation \(j_*j^*(A\boxtimes A)\to\Delta_* A\) takes inputs allowed poles at collision and gives a field supported on the diagonal. Transposing its two inputs gives skew-symmetry of the bracket. Its twofold composites on three inputs satisfy the Jacobi identity. In the ordered product above the coefficient at two points lives on *all* of \(X^2\), and \(H\) compares the two ways of multiplying three entries. [The two operations on a curve](#lie-and-operator-product)

For four degree-zero closed fields \(a,b,c,e\), start with \(((ab)c)e\). One path goes through \((ab)(ce)\) to \(a(b(ce))\). The other goes through \((a(bc))e\) and \(a((bc)e)\) to the same endpoint. Each step uses the comparison for three inputs. The four-input operation compares the two composites of these steps. [How one differential records the comparisons](#ch05-one-differential-encodes-all-associativity-homotopies)

At two points one can already see why the extension to a collision requires a choice. Hold the centre fixed, and let the difference of the two positions describe a small circle. If parallel transport on a fibre \(E\) around that circle is \(C\), its cellular cochain complex is \(E\xrightarrow{C-1}E\). For a one-dimensional complex fibre and \(C=-1\), this complex is acyclic. For \(C=1\), a copy of the fibre survives in each of degrees zero and one. There is also the possibility of placing a complex at the collision itself. Its restriction to the punctured disk vanishes. Knowing every value and every continuation at distinct points therefore leaves a question even when every continuation is trivial. One must specify what is placed on the diagonal and how it attaches to the surrounding fields. A coefficient at marked points must therefore allow both differentiation while the points move and restriction when some merge. In the differential-module description the latter operation is written \(\Delta^!\). [The normal link of a collision](#ch04-normal-link)

At two points write \(A_1\) for the complex on the curve and \(A_2\) for the complex on the space of pairs. Where the two points are distinct, the inputs have the external product \(A_1\boxtimes^{\mathbf L}A_1\). To multiply them one needs an arrow on all of \(X^2\),
\[
Rj_*j^*(A_1\boxtimes^{\mathbf L}A_1)\longrightarrow A_2,
\]
and an identification \(\Delta^!A_2\simeq A_1\) on the diagonal. The source extends the separated inputs and permits their poles. The target says which field their collision creates. To keep track of the empty input as well, specify an augmentation to the scalar coefficient at the empty configuration. Its kernel contains the positive-arity inputs. A word of three such inputs has two gaps at which it can be cut before or after a multiplication.

What makes this multiplication *chiral* is the geometry on which it acts. On a smooth complex algebraic curve \(X\), use complexes of right algebraic \(\mathcal D\)-modules on each \(X^n\). Their underlying sheaves are quasi-coherent, differential operators act in the marked positions, and the complexes have compatible extraordinary pullbacks along the diagonals that merge consecutive labels. The word \(1,\ldots,n\) records the order of multiplication. A cut after \(p\) separates two blocks. It is available on the open set \(U_{p,n-p}\) where each point of the first block is distinct from every point of the second. Points within a block may coincide. In the ordered word model, form the *bar construction* \(BA\) of an augmented coherent multiplication \(A\). It records strings of inputs and applies the products and their homotopies to consecutive entries. On degree-zero elements of the augmentation ideal of an ordinary associative algebra its product differential sends \([a|b|c]\) to \([ab|c]-[a|bc]\). The next differential gives the difference of the two triple products. For an associative chiral algebra in this model, cutting the string gives an equivalence on the separating open. Writing \(q=n-p\) and using \(\boxtimes\) for derived external tensor product, the factorization condition is
\[
(BA)_n|_{U_{p,q}}\xrightarrow{\simeq}\bigl((BA)_p\boxtimes(BA)_q\bigr)|_{U_{p,q}}.
\]

The equivalence identifies a word split into two separated clusters with the external product of their coefficients. In all arities an **associative chiral algebra** is an augmented \(E_1\)-algebra for the chosen convolution, with positive augmentation ideal and a bar coalgebra satisfying this factorization for every admissible division of the labels. Here \(E_1\) means the ordered products with their associativity homotopies. Convolution forms a product by grouping the inputs, taking their external product where the groups are disjoint and extending it to the full configuration. The choice of admissible groups matters.

Which divisions are admissible depends on the geometry. For a word in one fixed order, convolution uses consecutive cuts, and a monotone collision combines neighbours. The full ordered configuration space glues a chart for every total order and allows collision maps with ordered fibres. Its symmetric monoidal convolution sums over all ordered decompositions into subsets. Restrict to a single increasing chart and project onto the consecutive summands to recover the word coalgebra. This projection need not be an equivalence. One can see what a nonconsecutive collision contributes without constructing a coalgebra. Put \(u=z_1-z_3,\ v=z_2-z_3\) and consider \(du\wedge dv/[u(u-v)]\). Around \(u=v\) the first residue, in \(u-v\), is \(dv/v\), giving iterated residue \(1\). Around \(v=0\) first the form has no generic pole, giving \(0\). Around \(u=0\) first the coefficient is \(-dv/v\), giving \(-1\). The three flags sum to zero, the residue identity for the chiral Lie bracket. Equating the first two answers would erase the contribution from the first and third points. The associativity of consecutive cuts is a different comparison, between the two bracketings of an ordered product. [Associative products on configurations](#ch12-associative-products-on-configurations)

To put the pieces back together, form words of blocks, with a differential that splits one block by its coproduct. This reverse construction is called cobar. The positions determine which terms in the differential are available. Keep the fields in the order \(1,2,3\). When the first two points meet away from the third, the cut \(12\mid3\) is still available. When the last two meet away from the first, one can use \(1\mid23\). At a meeting of the first and third points every cut separates a pair at the same position. Both cuts fail along the entire diagonal \(z_1=z_3\), even where the middle point lies elsewhere. [The two separating opens for three points](#fig-ordered-cut-support)

How much of the multiplication survives the passage to cuts? Give the empty configuration coefficient \(\mathbb C\), with its specified augmentation on the algebra and coaugmentation on the coalgebra. The reduced coefficients then have positive arity. A word of length \(n\) admits at most \(n\) nonempty blocks, so the iterated reduced constructions terminate at each arity. For a smooth separated algebraic curve of finite type, the resulting inverse theorem concerns the sequence of coefficient complexes. Bar and *cobar* are inverse equivalences between connected augmented algebras and connected counital coaugmented coalgebras for consecutive cuts, with their full derived spaces of maps. For a factorizing coalgebra the reduced three-input cobar coefficient vanishes wherever \(z_1\ne z_3\). Along \(z_1=z_3\), its support depends on the coalgebra. To carry the construction through a collision, observe that a cut inside a block of labels being identified disappears, while a cut between two such blocks descends to the corresponding cut of the shorter word. A comparison with fields constructed from a different theory must in addition identify their vacuum, translations and collision operations. [Recovering the algebra from cuts](#ch05-ordered-cuts-give-a-finite-geometric-bar-construction)

There is a short reason why putting the cuts back gives the original object. A term in cobar of bar is a word of words. Flatten it to a string of \(r\) nonempty pieces. In the associated graded complex each gap is either inside one word or between two words. The differential which changes that choice gives the two copies of the same coefficient, joined by the identity, at every gap. For \(r>1\), pair terms at the first gap. They contract in pairs with opposite incidence signs. The single piece survives. At a configuration of \(n\) points there can be at most \(n\) nonempty pieces, so this calculation finishes in each arity even for a coefficient complex unbounded in both cohomological directions. It is this finiteness which allows one to say that maps and their homotopies also come back. There is no operation in the argument which assigns a state space to an arbitrary line or prescribes a value for an independent field at the collision.

## A symmetry on a pair of states {#opening-spectral-symmetry}

A different question arises when a symmetry acts on two independently prepared systems. Which current acts on their tensor product? If \(J_1\) and \(J_2\) have levels \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) and their mixed product is regular, their sum has double-pole coefficient \(\kappa_1+\kappa_2\). Writing \(H_\kappa\) for the algebra of a free current of level \(\kappa\), the simultaneous action comes from
\[
\begin{aligned}
H_{\kappa_1+\kappa_2}&\longrightarrow H_{\kappa_1}\otimes H_{\kappa_2},\\
J&\longmapsto J_1\otimes\mathbf1+\mathbf1\otimes J_2 .
\end{aligned}
\]
The direction and levels of this map are forced by the double pole. A unital field map from \(H_\kappa\) to the scalars would have to preserve it, so the regularity of scalar fields would force \(\kappa=0\). The augmentation at the empty configuration in the bar theorem belongs to the geometric coefficient system. In a chiral quantum group the coproduct must preserve the field products, give compatible actions on three systems and respect exchange when their positions move. These requirements concern the tensor product of systems. Cutting a string in the bar construction alone supplies no such action.

Let the two currents act on spaces \(M\) and \(N\). On \(M\otimes N\), their sum acts by \(J_m^{(1)}\otimes1+1\otimes J_m^{(2)}\). The mixed commutators vanish, giving the central coefficient \(m(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)\) when the other mode has index \(-m\). Combining a third current gives the same operator in either bracketing. Now place three fields at \(z+w,z,0\). Moving the first pair past the last field can also be done one field at a time. This compares exchanges at *two different separations*, \(z+w\) and \(z\). A rule for adding currents on a tensor product has supplied no such dependence on position.

The direction of the coproduct can already be seen on functions. If a system has a group-valued state and two states combine as \(g,h\mapsto gh\), an observable \(F\) on the result becomes the function \((g,h)\mapsto F(gh)\) on the pair. Applying this rule twice gives \(F((gh)k)=F(g(hk))\). This equality of readings on three inputs is coassociativity. For fields we also have to specify what crosses from one input to the other during multiplication.

To formulate this requirement for any associative chiral algebra, specify how two independent systems are combined *at the same positions*, writing their tensor product as \(A\diamond A'\). To multiply in it, one must interchange the middle two inputs before multiplying in the respective factors. In chiral geometry this interchange acts on the configuration extensions, on a common domain for their poles and supports. A **chiral coproduct** is then an algebra map \(D:A\to A\diamond A\), with coherent coassociativity and a counit to the unit of \(\diamond\), or a family which combines levels. For collision coefficients one can tensor the two bar coalgebras on the diagonal and transport back by cobar, obtaining \(A\diamond A'=\Omega(BA\otimes^! BA')\). To identify this product with the tensor of fields carrying an exchange, one still has to construct an interchange with those fields. The free current illustrates why cutting \(BA\) at separated positions and acting on a pair of systems solve different problems.

A simple source of position dependence is a contraction between generators. Take a polynomial algebra on commuting variables \(x_0,x_1,\ldots\) with translation \(Tx_n=x_{n+1}\). Let each act on a pair by the sum of its two actions. Assign two copies of \(x_0\) a contraction \(b/z\) at separation \(z\), and differentiate in its first position by \(\partial_z\) and in its second by \(-\partial_z\). The identity on the \(x_n\) and the simultaneous sign change \(x_n\mapsto-x_n\) preserve this rule. These levels are symmetries of the current algebra. They combine by composition. At the identity level the product on \(x_0,x_0\) is regular. At the sign-change level the contraction adds \(-2b/z\) to that same regular product. To combine levels one must know which generator crosses the other and with which contraction. [Primitive fields at two levels](#ch12-example-primitive-fields-at-two-levels)

There is a class in which this compatibility is a theorem. Take a Hopf algebra over \(\mathbb C\) with bijective antipode and a translation \(T\) that is both a derivation and a coderivation and satisfies \(\varepsilon T=0\), where \(\varepsilon\) is its counit. A unitary spectral coquasitriangular form \(\rho_z\) pairs two inputs in Laurent polynomials in their separation \(z\). It is multiplicative against the coproduct, differentiates under translation, and implements an exchange whose reverse is its inverse. For Hopf automorphisms \(\eta,\theta\) preserving the translation and the form, these data construct a field algebra \(V_\eta\) at each such level \(\eta\). Writing \(\Delta\) for the Hopf coproduct, the maps for combining levels form a coassociative family
\[
D_{\eta,\theta}=(\theta\otimes 1)\Delta:
V_{\eta\theta}\longrightarrow V_\eta\boxtimes_\rho V_\theta .
\]
The tensor product on the right uses \(\rho\) when a field in one factor passes a field in the other. With that contraction included, applying \(D\) to a product gives the product of the images, with its full dependence on \(z\). Exchanging the product with a third field is the composite of the two exchanges with its factors. This is the field hexagon. [The spectral field and coproduct](#ch12-a-spectral-construction-of-products-and-coproducts)

At the identity level the Hopf counit removes an empty factor from the product of systems. To test the coproduct on two insertions, write \(Y(a,z)b\) for the field of \(a\) evaluated on \(b\), and \(Y_{12}\) for the field on the two factors with their cross-contraction. Apply \(D=D_{\eta,\theta}\) to the state created when \(a\) approaches \(b\). Applying it first to the two inputs must give the same state, with the cross-contraction included. This requires
\[
D\bigl(Y_{\eta\theta}(a,z)b\bigr)=Y_{12}(Da,z)Db .
\]
Let \(\mathcal S_{ij}(u)\) exchange the fields in positions \(i,j\) at separation \(u\) in the field hexagon convention. Acting first on the first two states by \(Y(w)(a\otimes b)=Y(a,w)b\) gives the left-hand side. Exchanging the third state successively with them gives the right-hand side
\[
\begin{gathered}
\mathcal S(z)(Y(w)\otimes1)\\
=(Y(w)\otimes1)\mathcal S_{23}(z)\mathcal S_{13}(z+w).
\end{gathered}
\]
The rightmost factor acts first. Its argument \(z+w\) is expanded in nonnegative powers of the inner separation \(w\). The next factor uses \(z\), the distance from the second insertion to the third.

The hexagon tells us how to move a third field past a pair once we know the product of the pair. What can that product put inside the contour? To inspect the pair alone, now let \(w\) be the position held fixed and \(z\) the position of the other insertion. We expand at \(z-w=0\). For the free current, multiplying \(J(z)J(w)\) by \(z-w\) and taking the residue gave the scalar \(\kappa\mathbf1\). In the affine current algebra the currents \(J^x\) are labelled by elements \(x\) of a Lie algebra. The simple pole of \(J^x(z)J^y(w)\) is \(J^{[x,y]}(w)/(z-w)\), so the unweighted residue gives the current labelled by the Lie bracket. In both cases the commutator of two contour modes can be written using single current modes and the identity. What happens if a weighted contour returns a *pair* of fields at the same point?

For the universal \(W_3\) chiral algebra on the affine line, translation identifies the state spaces at different points. In the coordinate disk about \(w\), the product of two fields therefore depends on the difference \(z-w\). The resulting state space, vacuum, translation and local field products form its vertex algebra. Why does this algebra have generators of weights two and three? One can see both numbers in a matrix. Take a complex \(3\times3\) matrix \(M\) of trace zero, and let \(E_{ij}\) have a single \(1\) in row \(i\), column \(j\). Fix the entries \(M_{21}=M_{32}=1,\ M_{31}=0\). This keeps the two steps of the nilpotent matrix \(f=E_{21}+E_{32}\), which sends the first basis vector to the second, the second to the third and the third to zero. Let \(N\) be the group of upper triangular matrices with diagonal entries one. Which entries of \(M\) can we fix by a change of basis in \(N\)?

For \(n\in N\), write \(r=n_{12}\) and \(s=n_{23}\). Conjugation by \(n\) changes the diagonal \((d_1,d_2,d_3)\) of \(M\) to \((d_1+r,d_2-r+s,d_3-s)\). The first and last entries vanish precisely for \(r=-d_1,\ s=d_3\). The middle entry then vanishes because \(M\) has trace zero. With this choice of \(r,s\), set \(M'=nMn^{-1}\) and write \(x=M'_{12}\), \(y=M'_{23}\). The changes of basis that preserve the zero diagonal have the form \(1+hE_{13}\). Conjugation by this matrix changes \(x,y\) to \(x+h,y-h\). They agree precisely when \(h=(y-x)/2\). The constrained matrices with zero diagonal and equal entries immediately above it form a plane. The choices of \(r,s,h\) show that each \(N\)-orbit of constrained matrices meets this plane in exactly one point. On this plane let \(u\) be the common entry and \(v\) the upper-right entry.
\[
M(u,v)=
\begin{pmatrix}0&u&v\\1&0&u\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det(\xi I-M)=\xi^3-2u\xi-v
\]
Conjugation preserves traces, so the coordinates \(u=\operatorname{tr}(M^2)/4\) and \(v=\operatorname{tr}(M^3)/3\) can be read from the original matrix.

The point \(M(0,0)\) is the principal nilpotent \(f=E_{21}+E_{32}\). Which orbit does the plane cut across there? Let the full group \(\mathrm{SL}_3(\mathbb C)\) of determinant-one matrices act by conjugation on all trace-zero matrices. Its orbit through \(f\) consists of the matrices \(gfg^{-1}\) as \(g\) ranges over this group. The trace pairing identifies a trace-zero matrix \(M\) with the functional \(A\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(MA)\) on the trace-zero matrices. Replacing \(M\) by \(gMg^{-1}\) evaluates that functional at \(g^{-1}Ag\). This is the coadjoint action, so the conjugacy class of \(f\) is its coadjoint orbit. An infinitesimal change of basis in direction \(A\) moves \(f\) in direction \([A,f]\). Since \(f\) is a single Jordan block, the trace-zero matrices commuting with it are the span of \(f,f^2\). The orbit therefore has six tangent directions in the eight-dimensional space of trace-zero matrices.

Both traces defining \(u,v\) are constant along the orbit. On the displayed plane, moving from \(f\) in direction \(E_{12}+E_{23}\) changes \(u\) at unit rate and leaves \(v\) fixed. Moving in direction \(E_{13}\) changes \(v\) at unit rate and leaves \(u\) fixed. These two directions therefore complement the orbit tangent space at \(f\). The raising matrix \(e=2(E_{12}+E_{23})\) also has one Jordan block. Its trace-zero centralizer has basis \(e,e^2\), the two slice directions scaled by two and four. The plane through \(f\) in these directions is the principal *Slodowy slice*.

For \(\lambda\ne0\), conjugation by \(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,1,\lambda^{-1})\) followed by multiplying the matrix by \(\lambda\) fixes \(f\) and sends \((u,v)\) to \((\lambda^2u,\lambda^3v)\). The cubic coordinate \(v\) has weight three. A double pole in the product of two fields of this weight leaves weight \(3+3-2=4\), the weight of \(u^2\) on the slice. To see what becomes of these constraints on a curve, label each linear observable by a matrix \(A\) and put \(\ell_A(M)=\operatorname{tr}(AM)\). Its bracket with \(\ell_B\) is \(\{\ell_A,\ell_B\}=\ell_{[A,B]}\), the matrix Lie–Poisson bracket. In particular \(\ell_{E_{12}}=M_{21}\), \(\ell_{E_{23}}=M_{32}\), and their bracket is \(\ell_{E_{13}}=M_{31}\). The two trace polynomials commute with every \(\ell_A\) because they are invariant under conjugation. Thus the two surviving coordinates have zero finite Poisson bracket.

What changes when a matrix entry is a *field*? At each position take a current \(J^A\) for every trace-zero matrix \(A\), linear in \(A\). At level \(k\) their polar product is
\[
J^A(z)J^B(w)\sim
\frac{k\operatorname{tr}(AB)\mathbf1}{(z-w)^2}
+\frac{J^{[A,B]}(w)}{z-w}.
\]
Weight the contour by a holomorphic function \(g(z)\) and integrate once counterclockwise about \(w\) with measure \(dz/(2\pi i)\). The simple pole gives \(g(w)J^{[A,B]}(w)\). At the double pole only the linear term of \(g(z)\) contributes, giving \(k\operatorname{tr}(AB)g'(w)\mathbf1\). For a constant parameter the action is the matrix commutator. When the change of basis varies along the disk, its first derivative enters through the central term. Fix the same three linear observables now on the currents for \(E_{12},E_{23},E_{13}\), with values \(1,1,0\), and divide out the corresponding position-dependent changes of basis. At the quantum level the constraints and gauge identifications are imposed together by a differential. For each of the three matrices, in the order just given, introduce odd fields \(b_i,\gamma^i\) of degrees \(-1,+1\). Their contraction is \(b_i(z)\gamma^j(w)\sim\delta_i^j/(z-w)\), and the like fields have regular products. Colons denote the normal product. To form it, subtract the singular contractions and take the regular coefficient at coincidence. With \(J_i\) the three constrained currents, the differential \(Q\) acts by the residue of the field
\[
:\gamma^1(J_1-\mathbf1):
+:\gamma^2(J_2-\mathbf1):
+:\gamma^3J_3:
-:\gamma^1\gamma^2 b_3: .
\]
The last term records \([E_{12},E_{23}]=E_{13}\) and sends \(\gamma^3\) to \(-:\gamma^1\gamma^2:\). The potentially nonzero commutator of the first two constraints cancels against this term, and the trace pairing vanishes between strictly upper triangular matrices. Hence \(Q^2=0\). For example \(Qb_3=J_3\), so the third constraint holds in cohomology. The fields of degree zero killed by \(Q\), modulo \(Q\) of fields of degree minus one, form the principal quantum Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction of the affine vacuum algebra. Here the vacuum is the state of a disk without an insertion. Starting with the universal affine algebra imposes the current and vacuum identities and leaves possible further relations in particular representations for later. [The affine reduction, §5.1, Proposition 5.4 and Lemma 5.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.00138#page=30)

To find the fields that survive the constraints, filter their products by total order of derivatives. This is the decreasing Li filtration. In the resulting commutative differential algebra, the constraint equations and their derivatives cut out the jets of the constrained matrices. A jet records Taylor coefficients at one point. Triangular changes of basis remove the orbit coordinates order by order, leaving the independent Taylor coefficients of \(u,v\). The reduction theorem identifies the algebra of these Taylor coefficients with the associated graded of the degree-zero cohomology. Thus \(u,v\) lift to closed fields whose derivatives and ordered products span the universal algebra. Both the normal square of the weight-two field and its second derivative have weight four. What coefficients do they have in the double pole of the weight-three field with itself? [The slice, its jets and the filtered reduction, Theorems 5.7–5.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.00138#page=32)

To evaluate the contour with weight \(z-w\), we need a representative of the cubic field whose contractions we can compute. For \(k\ne-3\) choose a square root of \(k+3\) and set \(\alpha=(k+2)/\sqrt{k+3}\). Take two free abelian currents and three linear combinations with sum zero. These fields \(H_i\) have contractions \((\delta_{ij}-1/3)/(z-w)^2\). Form the normally ordered product of three differential operators, moving derivatives to the right by \(D H_i=H_i D+\alpha\partial H_i\), where \(\partial\) differentiates the insertion coordinate. The polynomial identity
\[
:(D+H_1)(D+H_2)(D+H_3):
=D^3+B_2D+B_3
\]
defines its coefficients even when \(\alpha=0\). The \(D^2\) term vanishes since \(\sum_i H_i=0\). With derivatives suppressed, \(B_2\) and \(B_3\) are the pairwise sum of products and the triple product of the three diagonal currents, the quadratic and cubic characteristic invariants. With derivatives retained, moving \(D\) past the last two factors adds \(\alpha(\partial H_2+2\partial H_3)\) to \(B_2\). The injective quantum Miura map realizes the reduced fields by these coefficients. The stress field is \(L=-B_2\), and the cubic field corrected to transform with weight three is \(X=B_3+(\alpha/2)\partial L\). The fourth pole of the stress tensor with itself has coefficient \(c/2\), defining its central charge. Contraction gives \(c=2-24\alpha^2\). At \(\alpha=0\) we have \(c=2\) and \(X=:H_1H_2H_3:\). In \(X(z)X(w)\), contracting just \(H_1(z)\) with \(H_1(w)\) contributes \(\tfrac23:H_2H_3H_2H_3:(w)\) to the weighted residue. The four remaining currents are normally ordered together. Contracting two pairs instead gives a factor \((z-w)^{-4}\), so the second Taylor coefficient of the remaining current at \(z\) also contributes to the double pole. Multiplying \(X\) by a constant scales every pole of its self-product by the square of that constant. The sixth pole is a scalar we can compute by pairing every current on the left with one on the right. Pairing all three like labels contributes \((2/3)^3=8/27\). Swapping two labels keeps one like pair and contributes \(2/27\) in each of three ways. A cyclic permutation pairs unlike labels throughout and contributes \(-1/27\) in each of two ways. The six pairings therefore give \(4/9\). More generally the total contraction is \(c(22+5c)/144\). The sixth pole fixes the squared scale of the cubic field for generic charge. At \(c=0\) it vanishes, but the fourth pole of \(X(z)X(w)\) still has coefficient \((22+5c)L/24\). Requiring the fourth pole of the normalized field to be \(2L\) gives the same scale there. [Injectivity of the Miura map, Theorem 5.17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.00138#page=38) · [The trace-free ordered product, Corollary 5.20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.00138#page=39)

To measure these two fields by contours, first take \(22+5c\ne0\) and use the conventional spin-three generator \(W=\sqrt{48/(22+5c)}\,X\). Integrating \(L(z)\) around an insertion with weight \(f(z)\) generates the infinitesimal coordinate change with vector field \(f(z)\partial_z\). For \(W\) this gives \(f(w)\partial W(w)+3f'(w)W(w)\). A constant weight translates the insertion. The weight \(z-w\) dilates about it and gives \(3W(w)\). This transformation law is what it means for \(W\) to be *primary of conformal weight three*. Here and below \(\partial\) differentiates a field in its insertion coordinate.

Write \(t=z-w\), evaluate the fields on the right at \(w\), and use \(\sim\) for equality of pole terms. The stress tensor has weight two. Its own coordinate transformation has an extra scalar term. The coefficient of the fourth pole is half the central charge \(c\). With \(\mathbf1\) the field of the empty insertion, the first two products are
\[
\begin{aligned}
L(z)L(w)&\sim \frac{c\mathbf1}{2t^4}
                 +\frac{2L}{t^2}+\frac{\partial L}{t},\\
L(z)W(w)&\sim \frac{3W}{t^2}+\frac{\partial W}{t}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Subtract the displayed poles from \(L(z)L(w)\) and take the constant term in \(t\). The resulting field, written \(:LL:\), is the *normal product* of the two stress tensors. It has weight four, exactly the weight a double pole can leave in a product of two weight-three fields. For \(22+5c\ne0\), put \(\beta=16/(22+5c)\) and \(\Lambda=:LL:-3\partial^2L/10\). Normalize \(W\) so that its self-product is
\[
\begin{aligned}
W(z)W(w)\sim{}&\frac{c\mathbf1}{3t^6}+\frac{2L}{t^4}
                    +\frac{\partial L}{t^3}\\
 &+\frac{2\beta\Lambda+\tfrac3{10}\partial^2L}{t^2}
   +\frac{\beta\partial\Lambda+\tfrac1{15}\partial^3L}{t}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The counterclockwise contour with weight \(t\) and measure \(dz/(2\pi i)\) now returns the entire field \(2\beta\Lambda+3\partial^2L/10\). Its quadratic part is \(2\beta:LL:\). In the earlier example \(\alpha=0\), one has \(c=2\) and \(2\beta=1\). The term \(-3\partial^2L/10\) in \(\Lambda\) then cancels \(+3\partial^2L/10\). The same contour returns precisely \(:LL:\). The coefficient and its accompanying derivatives are fixed by the consistency of successive products in this normalization. [Derivation from the Jacobi identity, Example 3.13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math-ph/0312042#page=10)

These three singular products present the universal \(W_3\) vertex algebra on a coordinate disk. Its states are made by inserting the two fields, their derivatives and their iterated normal products into the vacuum \(\mathbf1\), the state with no insertion. A field acting on the vacuum is regular at the origin and its constant term is the state it creates. Translation takes this state to the state of the differentiated field. Universality means that specifying two even fields with these products in another vertex algebra determines a unique field map from this one. A particular representation can impose further relations among its states. [States, fields and translation](#vertex-algebra)

To test whether the quadratic field can be expressed using single modes, keep the normalization regular as \(c\) varies. Put \(a=(22+5c)/16\) and use \(U=\sqrt3\,X\). For \(a\ne0\) choose compatible square roots so \(U=\sqrt a\,W\). The \(LU\) product still expresses weight three. Multiplying the \(WW\) product by \(a\) makes every coefficient polynomial in \(c\), since \(a\beta=1\). These relations define the generator \(U\) also at \(a=0\). At \(a=0\) the singular part of \(U(z)U(w)\) is \(2\Lambda/t^2+\partial\Lambda/t\). This specialization retains the universal vacuum before any further quotient. A simple quotient can impose additional vacuum relations. [The regular presentation and its basis, equations (3.3)–(3.5)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.06255#page=4)

Expand \(L(z)=\sum_m L_m z^{-m-2}\) and \(U(z)=\sum_m U_m z^{-m-3}\). Thus \(U_{-3}\mathbf1\) creates the spin-three state and \(U_{-1}\mathbf1=0\). Acting by \(U_{-1}\) on that state picks out the double pole. The state of \(:LL:\) is \(L_{-2}^2\mathbf1\), two successive stress insertions. The state of \(\partial^2L\) is \(2L_{-4}\mathbf1\), obtained by differentiating one insertion twice. Substitution in the double pole gives
\[
[U_{-1},U_{-3}]\mathbf1
=2L_{-2}^2\mathbf1+\tfrac35(a-2)L_{-4}\mathbf1 .
\]
Ordered creation words form a basis of this universal vacuum at every finite \(c\), including \(c=-22/5\). In weight four, applying a single mode of either original generator to the vacuum gives only \(L_{-4}\mathbf1\) or \(U_{-4}\mathbf1\), up to scalars. Their span misses \(L_{-2}^2\mathbf1\). The vacuum experiment rules out closing these commutators on the original modes and the identity. If a compatible spectral exchange is given, its hexagon must transport the whole product, including that two-mode state. Constructing such an exchange for these fields is a separate question. [The spin-three calculation](#ch12-nonlinear-fields-the-w3-example)

The same contour decides what information is needed to map these fields into an ordinary vertex algebra concentrated in cohomological degree zero. Suppose the proposed images of \(L,W\) are \(\ell,v\). Pair them with two independent odd fields \(\tau,\varpi\), one for each generator, to form \(q=\ell\otimes\tau+v\otimes\varpi\). The coefficient of \((z-w)^0\) in \(\ell(z)\ell(w)\) is \(:\ell\ell:\). For this coefficient to contribute to the residue of \(q(z)q(w)\), the paired product \(\tau(z)\tau(w)\) must supply a simple pole. Regular exterior products would give zero for this contribution. For \(22+5c\ne0\) the dual fields instead obey
\[
\tau(z)\tau(w)\sim \frac{2\beta:\varpi\,\partial\varpi:(w)}{z-w}.
\]
The pole multiplies the regular product and supplies the missing quadratic term. It also gives a derivative correction when the order of normal products in the dual is reversed. Transposing the linear and scalar relations gives a degree-one derivation \(d\) and a degree-two field \(w_B\), the curvature. Its residue acts by \(d^2=(w_B)_{(0)}\). Writing \(q_{(0)}q\) for the residue of the self-product of \(q\), the condition on \(\ell,v\) is that \(dq+\tfrac12q_{(0)}q+w_B\) be a total derivative in the tensor product (with \(w_B\) inserted as \(\mathbf1\otimes w_B\)). It holds exactly when \(L\mapsto\ell,\ W\mapsto v\) defines a field map. The same construction applies in characteristic zero to universal vertex algebras freely generated by finitely many even fields of half-integer weights at least one with homogeneous quadratic, linear and scalar brackets. For the regular universal algebra at every finite \(c\), the completed mode construction gives an adjunction and a bar complex that computes derived maps between smooth modules, with their composition. ## Which representations cross an interface {#opening-interfaces}

Take a system over a field \(k\) with one sector on the left and two independent sectors on the right. The algebras are \(B=k\) and \(A=k\times k\). A module over \(A\) is a pair \((N_1,N_2)\), since the idempotents \((1,0),(0,1)\) project onto its two parts. An interface carrying \(P=(k,0)\) on the right sends a complex \(M\) to \((M,0)\). Passing it back recovers \(M\). Starting with \((N_1,N_2)\) gives the round trip \((N_1,N_2)\mapsto N_1\mapsto(N_1,0)\). Thus we can recover every complex sent across this interface and still miss an entire sector on the other side.

For a general \(A\)-\(B\) bimodule \(P\), passage and return are the functors \(F(M)=P\otimes_B^{\mathbf L}M\) and \(G(N)=R\operatorname{Hom}_A(P,N)\). Under the tensor–Hom adjunction the identity of \(FM\) gives the unit \(M\to GFM\), and the identity of \(GN\) gives the counit \(FGN\to N\). For degree-zero inputs the unit sends \(m\) to \(p\mapsto p\otimes m\), and the counit sends \(p\otimes f\) to \(f(p)\). On resolutions, interchanging homogeneous inputs of degrees \(i,j\) contributes the sign \((-1)^{ij}\). The unit compares \(M\) with its round trip. The counit asks whether evaluation recovers \(N\). When every unit is an equivalence, \(F\) is fully faithful and evaluation recovers precisely the objects in the essential image of \(F\). [The unit and counit](#ch06-definition-unit-and-counit)

For currents there are infinitely many modes to pass across the interface. What takes the place of the one-sector module \(P\)? For a finite-dimensional simple complex Lie algebra \(\mathfrak g\), let \(A\) be the affine mode algebra at level \(s-h^\vee\), where \(h^\vee\) is the dual Coxeter number and \(s\in\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb Q\). Start with a vector killed by the strictly positive currents \(t\mathfrak g[t]\), where \(t\) is the local coordinate. The constant currents act freely on the lowest-energy space of the induced module
\[
W=A\otimes_{U(t\mathfrak g[t])}\mathbb C .
\]
To keep the relations killing that vector, resolve \(W\) by exterior powers of the positive currents. Which algebra acts from the other side of this resolution? The polynomial differential graded line algebra \(B=Y_{-s}(\mathfrak g)\) acts on its right by endomorphisms. Composition reverses the order of a right action, with the graded interchange sign. For algebraic affine modules this gives a quasi-isomorphism from \(B\) to the graded opposite of the endomorphism algebra of the resolution. Passing to smooth modules requires a check on how many coefficients of a current enter a composite.

How much of a positive current can act on a given vector? In a smooth affine module each vector \(v\) is killed by \(t^N\mathfrak g[t]\) for some \(N\). The current acts on \(v\) through its coefficients of \(t,\ldots,t^{N-1}\). Keeping these coefficients is the Taylor cutoff of order \(N\). At order two only the linear term can contribute. Yet a negative mode \(xt^{-1}\) has bracket \([x,y]t\) with \(yt^2\). An input discarded at order two can therefore contribute to an output at order two. On the resolution, this operator can be evaluated from order three to order two. For a finite diagram of operators, choose one input order large enough for every composite to reach the chosen output order. The maps can then be compared on a common resolution, compatibly as the order increases. [Smooth affine modules](#ch06-definition-smooth-affine-module) · [The action on the resolution](#ch06-affine-currents-and-the-algebra-acting-on-their-resolution)

Assume that these actions, with their higher homotopies, give \(W\) a coherent right \(B\)-action in the smooth derived category and that the prescribed comparison of its smooth derived endomorphisms holds. Assume also the enhanced tensor–Hom adjunction between induction and taking derived maps out of this smooth realization of \(W\). Under these hypotheses the unit \(M\to GFM\) is an equivalence for every unbounded \(B\)-complex \(M\). The image of induction consists precisely of the smooth affine complexes generated by \(W\) under shifts, cones, coproducts and retracts, including energy shifts in the graded convention. [The smooth adjunction](#eq-6-1) · [The full-unit theorem](#ch06-conditional-full-unit-theorem)

How can a finite calculation prove this for an unbounded complex? Let \(I\) be the positive-weight ideal of \(B\), \(R=U(\mathfrak g)\), and \(D\) the number of positive roots. A surviving positive-weight factor gives a strict step among reflection labels. Their order is the Bruhat order, in which a reduced word for the lesser element occurs as a subword of a reduced word for the greater. At this shifted level the reflection group in each block is finite and a chain has at most \(D\) such steps. The derived tensor power of \(I\) over \(R\) of length \(D+1\) vanishes, while the power of length \(D\) can survive. For \(\mathfrak{sl}_3\) one has a nonzero cube and a zero fourth power. This gives a finite resolution of the unit comparison by comparisons on coefficients inflated from \(R\). It also explains why one must ask again at rational shifted level. At raw \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) level \(-3\), there is an infinite direct-limit complex killed by reduction to \(R\) which still passes through the interface and returns intact. At the critical raw level \(-h^\vee\), assuming its smooth realization and the unit theorem on complexes bounded below in energy, there are nonzero complexes killed by the affine realization itself. An independently defined physical induction and restriction would have to realize these maps on their original domains and carry the product of two lines through the interface. [The affine interface](#affine-reconstruction-scope)

The interface describes which modules come back. Could one recover the *field product* from modules if one knew how the modules varied as their positions came together? For an ordinary unital chiral algebra on a smooth separated algebraic curve, take the complete families of ordinary modules with arbitrary classical parameter schemes. Inserting the empty unit in those families gives the vacuum on all configurations. The two-point residue of that vacuum retrieves the original chiral multiplication, including its extension to a collision. Classical parameters matter. A module supported at the origin can have zero ordinary fibre and a nonzero first derived fibre. In the coordinate model \(\delta_0=\mathbb C[u]\), the transverse parameter acts as \(-d/du\). Its cokernel is zero and its kernel is the constants. Ordinary restriction sees the cokernel, whereas derived restriction retains both. This round trip therefore proves something different from the equivalence on consecutive-cut coefficient sequences. It starts with the complete ordinary family, including the collision information that the open configuration alone does not specify. ## Joining two insertions {#opening-joining}

Bring two insertions together in the Ising model. If each carries the spin sector \(\sigma\), a contour around both can see either the vacuum \(\mathbf1\) or a second sector \(\psi\). This is the fusion rule \(\sigma\boxtimes\sigma=\mathbf1\oplus\psi\), and \(\psi\boxtimes\psi=\mathbf1\). Suppose one tried to assign ordinary finite-dimensional vector spaces to these sectors, taking sums to direct sums, fusion to tensor product, and the vacuum to the scalar line. The second rule forces \(\dim\psi=1\), and the first would give \((\dim\sigma)^2=2\). No such integer dimension exists. What one *can* assign to an insertion is an array of vector spaces, one for each possible incoming and outgoing sector.

For the simple Virasoro minimal models at coprime integers \(2\le p<q\), let \(L_i\), \(i\in I\), be the simple sectors and allow their finite direct sums. A three-point amplitude must respect changes of coordinate generated by the stress tensor at the three marks. These are the rational Ward identities. The fusion product represents their solutions with the relations of the simple algebra imposed and the third sector paired at infinity with its dual, taken weight space by weight space. To record the channels for \(M\), keep the space of ways in which \(L_i\) occurs when \(M\) fuses with \(L_j\). This gives
\[
\mathcal F(M)_{ij}=\operatorname{Hom}(L_i,M\boxtimes L_j).
\]
The algebra \(B_0=\mathbb C^I\) has an idempotent \(e_j\) for each sector. In \(\mathcal F(M)\otimes_{B_0}\mathcal F(N)\), the adjacent idempotents satisfy \(e_je_k=0\) unless \(j=k\). Precisely the composable channels survive. Composition with the associator gives the tensor comparison, and semisimplicity makes it invertible. The resulting finite weak Hopf algebra acts on these fibres. Its tensor unit is \(B_0\), and the coproduct of its identity projects an ordinary tensor onto the matching end labels. [The two ends of a channel](#ch13-finite-rational-categories-and-a-non-scalar-base)

What if the sector on a separating circle takes continuously many values? For positive principal Virasoro representations at real \(c>1\), the intermediate label is \(p>0\). Its lowest-energy state \(\Omega_p\) has weight \(h_p=(c-1)/24+p^2\). The first excitation is \(L_{-1}\Omega_p\). With the lowest state normalized to one, its bilinear pairing with itself is \(2h_p\), since \([L_1,L_{-1}]=2L_0\). Thus two three-point amplitudes with values \(u_1,v_1\) on that state contribute \(u_1v_1/(2h_p)\) when the two circles are sewn. At higher levels one contracts with the inverse of the full matrix of pairings, sums over the levels and integrates the intermediate labels with their spectral measure.

On the sphere marked at \(0,z,1,\infty\), cut around \(0,z\) or around \(z,1\). In the first cut, two lowest states of label \(1\) at \(c=25\) couple to the lowest state of label \(1/2\) with a coefficient proportional to \(z^{5/4-2-2}=z^{-11/4}\). One counterclockwise turn of \(z\) multiplies it by \(i\). At positive ordered positions (\(0<z<1\) here), take the real logarithm of the separation at each vertex. Continue all these logarithms along the same path of configurations. Fix the three-point normalization, spectral measure and local coordinates at the four punctures. For algebraic external states the normalized boundary hypothesis requires each channel sum to converge in its own disk and give a square-integrable vector in the intermediate momentum. The specified real unitary change of channel must carry one vector to the other on this lift. Iteration also needs the two-radius Gaussian bound on the sum of squared kernel entries at every triple of positive momenta, with descendant tails tending uniformly to zero in norm on smaller compact sets. Place the inputs at \(0,a_2,\ldots,a_d\) with \(0<|a_2|<\cdots<|a_d|\). On a compact set \(K\) in this logarithmic cover, fix the output weight \(e^{sE}\), where \(E\) is conformal energy and \(s<-\log|a_d|\) throughout \(K\). Under these hypotheses a finite input exponent \(t_K\) suffices for the left-associated sewing to be holomorphic and Hilbert–Schmidt, meaning that its squared matrix entries in orthonormal bases have finite sum. If the normalized four-point changes satisfy the pentagon on positive labels, their product takes one continued tree sewing to the other on the common domain. It acts unitarily on the retained intermediate labels and as the identity on the representation at each final momentum. Each tree series starts in its own region of convergence. [The four-point hypothesis](#ch10-definition-normalized-boundary-hypothesis) · [Radial estimates and finite sewings](#ch10-finite-uncut-sewings)

One might try to feed the result of such a sewing straight into the next operation as an elementary input. In the positive entire-energy source, even a collision of two highest vectors can fail that test after any fixed finite propagation. Its primary coefficient decays at best exponentially in \(h_p\), while the source tests *all* positive exponential weights. There is a simpler algebraic instance of the same issue. The series \(\sum_{n\ge0}q^n\lambda^{-n^2}\) is defined with coefficients rational in the shape variable \(\lambda\). Setting \(\lambda=zq\) gives outer exponents \(n-n^2\), which have no lower bound. Thus agreement of the integrands on separated points, or convergence for one prescribed sewing, leaves a genuine domain question when a cluster is used again. A common coefficient space for both compositions is part of the comparison. [The domain of a collision](#ch10-collision-and-the-two-conditions-for-descent)

## From an interior operation to a centre {#opening-central-actions}

So far the strength of a contour pole has been an input. What term in the action of an interior field could determine the boundary level \(\kappa\) measured on the first disk? Take an abelian theory on the half-space with complex coordinate \(z\) and height \(t\ge0\). In this holomorphic–topological theory the differential contains \(d'=\bar\partial+d_t\), while differentiation in \(z\) acts on fields. At degree zero, a partial connection \(a\) has components in the \(d\bar z,dt\) directions and a field \(b\) carries one \(dz\) factor. Write \(\partial=dz\wedge\partial_z\). The quadratic action has density
\[
 b\wedge d'a+\frac{\lambda}{2}a\wedge\partial a .
\]
Impose zero tangential value for \(a\) at \(t=0\) and leave the tangential value of \(b\) free. The full complex includes the infinitesimal gauge transformations and their antifields. Contracting a compactly supported profile in the normal direction gives the boundary current. A counterclockwise contour with weight \(z-w\) and measure \(dz/(2\pi i)\) picks out its double-pole coefficient \(-\hbar\lambda\), with the sign of the displayed action. Thus varying the bulk coefficient \(\lambda\) changes a specified boundary reading. The corresponding classical operation is a singular Poisson bracket of commuting fields, called a *coisson* bracket. Bracketing with a fixed field differentiates the product in the other entry. The three-field residues obey Jacobi. There is a limit to freely moving the bulk insertion toward the boundary. The derivative of the step function ordering two heights is a delta function at equal height. Its contribution to the Ward identity is a contact term, so the support of the test enters the comparison. [The mixed half-space](#ch09-a-boundary-current-from-a-mixed-field-theory)

Holography asks which operations and states on a boundary can be obtained from a field theory in the space it bounds. In a free holomorphic–topological half-space, a test field supported strictly above the boundary has zero boundary restriction. Multiply it by a boundary input before integrating out the transverse fields and there can be a contraction. On polynomial current states \(f(p_1,p_2,\ldots)\), a test tuned to harmonic \(j\) acts by \(\partial f/\partial p_j\) at quantum current level one. Each occurrence of \(p_j\) gives one possible contraction. This computes an interior action on the boundary cochains. To determine a state one must also specify its preparation.

What single object could receive *every* such compatible action? For an ordinary algebra \(A\), a natural operation on all left modules is determined by its value on the regular module. If that value on \(1\) is \(z\), naturality under the maps \(A\to M\) sending \(1\) to \(m\) gives \(m\mapsto zm\). On the regular module, \(A\)-linearity sends \(a\) to \(az\), while naturality under right multiplication sends it to \(za\). Thus \(z\) lies in the ordinary centre. Passing to complexes gives the derived centre \(R\operatorname{Hom}_{A\otimes A^{\mathrm{op}}}(A,A)\). On a curve an insertion has a position. One can bring it to a second insertion and then split the surrounding configuration. A recipient for such actions must compare both operations, their orders and their homotopies. A scalar in the ordinary centre has no argument in which to record a moving point.

Take two orthogonal idempotents \(p_1,p_2\) with \(1=p_1+p_2\). Multiplication by \(z=\lambda_1p_1+\lambda_2p_2\) acts by \(\lambda_i\) on \(p_i\), and its value on \(1\) recovers \(z\). With two marked positions there are also two possibilities for the *positions*: two separate singleton blocks, or one block containing both labels on the diagonal. Put a colour \(p_i\) on each block and merge blocks in the differential using the chiral product. In general the blocks run through the finite partitions of the marks. This construction is the main-diagonal Chevalley coalgebra. Can evaluation on the boundary unit still recover an action when the merging of these blocks has to be respected?

For this two-colour boundary, take a parameter coalgebra obtained by that construction from any unbounded differential graded chiral algebra on the affine line in characteristic zero with a closed horizontal unit. This means that its vacuum is killed by both the differential and translation and satisfies the unit identities. Choose a coherent pointing, a map into the parameter coalgebra specifying a vacuum compatible with its cuts. A character is a map to the boundary coalgebra carrying this pointing to the boundary unit up to specified homotopy. Multiplying its value by a boundary input gives an action. Conversely, insert the *boundary* unit in an action while leaving the parameter free. These two operations are inverse equivalences of the full pointed mapping spaces, for every such pointing. A recipient for arbitrary bulk actions would have to accept parameters beyond this family. [An action recovered from its unit](#ch07-recognition-on-chevalley-coalgebras)

Could a single boundary object receive each bulk action by a unique map, with all the homotopies of that choice included? This is the holomorphic–topological centre conjecture. For a unital holomorphic factorization algebra \(\mathcal A\) on a smooth complex \(d\)-fold, with specified supported density coefficients, there should be a mixed factorization algebra \(\mathcal Z_{\mathrm{diag}}(\mathcal A)\), topological in one real direction and holomorphic in \(d\) complex directions. It is to act unitally at each point and be terminal among the diagonal actions preserving the coefficients, collisions, factorization and supports. If it exists, any such action of a bulk \(\mathcal B\) factors through it by a map \(\beta:\mathcal B\to\mathcal Z_{\mathrm{diag}}(\mathcal A)\), with a contractible space of choices. The independent assertion that a given physical bulk *is* this centre asks whether its own map \(\beta\) is an equivalence. The category in the existence question is differentiable. For the prescribed algebraic pointed-action functor of the nonzero-level Heisenberg coalgebra, representability already fails. It is open whether that obstruction persists in the differentiable setting. [Pointed actions and collision compatibility](#problem-universal-centres)

What then does an observer following a line encounter? Two small insertions on it may be composed in their order along the line. A local operator can approach the line from a transverse complex direction. Describing the line by a module should preserve both experiments, including their dependence on separation. To extract possible line operators, start with coefficients \(F\) that can be multiplied in the transverse geometry and augmented to the empty coefficient. The line-operator conjecture assumes that their convolution cobar realizes the local field algebra, including its vacuum, product and insertion domains. Split the transverse inputs by the bar construction and keep the coefficient at one position. Realize it in complete complexes by a functor \(\mathsf R\) carrying the tensor products, their unit and the bar realization to the corresponding ones. If continuous duality transposes the completed tensor powers used by the operations, one can form
\[
\mathcal Y=\mathsf R\bigl((\operatorname{Bar}^{!}F)_{\{1\}}\bigr)^\vee_{\mathrm{cont}} .
\]
The conjecture gives this algebra the structure of a shifted differential graded Yangian and identifies lines, under matching finiteness conditions, with its admissible continuous modules. Line fusion is to be tensor product via the spectral coproduct. The comparison should carry translation, the vacuum line and the local insertions at a line to their module operations. Under bar duality the degree-minus-one bracket of local fields should correspond to the degree-one twist of the coproduct. The abelian sum of levels gives a case where one can write the action on two states. The nonrational affine theorem gives a case where the original unit on complexes is invertible. Combining these conclusions for a physical line would require the same interface to preserve the two orders of tensor product on their actual domains. [Lines, products and geometric insertions](#problem-chiral-quantum-groups)

## What is retained at an open end {#opening-open-ends}

Can one miss a global field even when the local contractions agree? Take two odd fields \(b,c\) with \(b(z)c(w)\sim1/(z-w)\). The contour of \(b\) with constant weight returns \(1\) on \(c\). Differentiating in \(w\) turns the pole into a double pole, so that contour returns zero on \(\partial c\). The subalgebra generated by \(b,\partial c\) loses the undifferentiated \(c\). This is the missing field in a comparison on D3 branes at rank one.

On a stack of \(N\) D3 branes, restrict the protected operators on the four-dimensional worldvolume to a complex line. One can first impose the gauge constraints on polynomial fields. In the relative gauge complex, one removes the constant gauge ghosts, requires invariance under the gauge currents and takes degree-zero cohomology of the differential imposing the constraints. Alternatively, on the stable part of the bosonic moment equation \([X,Y]+\gamma\beta=0\) one may first divide out the changes of basis. Stability means that the vector \(\gamma\) generates the \(N\)-dimensional space under \(X,Y\). The quotient is the Hilbert scheme of length-\(N\) subschemes of the auxiliary plane, where one now takes the torus-finite global chiral sections and specializes the quantum parameter to one. The polynomial algebra injects into this space. Its image is the kernel of the contour \(\chi^-_{(0)}\) of an odd weight-one field. A global weight-zero section \(\tau\) satisfies \(\chi^-_{(0)}\tau=N\). Dividing the contour by \(N\) recovers the rank-one test on the missing factor. A bulk action must determine on which of these two spaces it acts. [The polynomial and global brane operators](#ch14-d3-branes-chiral-operators-and-hilbert-scheme-sections)

With two surface defects there is a second question. Does their order enter what an insertion measures? The Miura product used to construct the spin-three field makes this question calculable for independent surface currents \(J_1,J_2\). Move the derivatives to the right in \((a\partial+J_1)(a\partial+J_2)\). Its constant coefficient is \(J_1J_2+a\partial J_2\). Reversing the factors differentiates \(J_1\) instead. For the M5 system, splitting an ordered product of \(N_1+N_2\) such factors defines a map from its coefficient algebra to the tensor product of the two shorter ones. Associativity of differential-operator composition gives coassociativity of this rank-addition map. Identifying it with fusion of the actual five-dimensional defects requires their quantization, Ward identities and the normalization of the mixed insertion. What information can survive on their common interface? [Ordered surface factors](#ch14-surface-fusion-and-the-miura-product)

If the algebra \(R\) acts at each end through \(R\to A\), join two copies by resolving one end as an \(R\)-module and tensoring with the other. The resulting complex \(A\otimes_R^{\mathbf L}A\) keeps relations at the common interface. A surjection gives \(A\) in degree zero. What can survive in negative degrees? For the quiver \(1\xrightarrow a2\xrightarrow b1\) with \(ab=0\) in traversal order, kill vertex 2. The resolution at vertex 1 has projectives at vertices \(1,2,1\). After tensoring with the remaining simple module it becomes \(k\to0\to k\) in degrees \(-2,-1,0\). The algebraic M2 particle junction exhibits this same separation for \(N\ge1\), nonzero numerical \(\hbar\) and zero Cherednik deformation. Its coupling sends a Weyl observable to the sum of its actions on the \(N\) particles. For position and conjugate operators the commutator of the two sums is \(N\hbar\,1\), since operators on different particles commute. The degree-minus-one cohomology, \(\operatorname{Tor}_1\), vanishes. In degree minus two, evaluating a sewing class on the scalar part of a lifted Weyl commutator maps \(\operatorname{Tor}_2\) onto the full particle algebra, with a bimodule splitting. It detects the flux left by joining the two ends. Identifying this cap with an insertion in the five-dimensional interval is a further comparison. [What the joined interface retains](#ch14-what-a-joined-interface-remembers)

Over \(\mathbb F_q\), how many ways can a plane be assembled from two lines? Every extension splits, yet the choice of a line *inside* the plane gives \((q^2-1)/(q-1)=q+1\) possibilities. Hall multiplication counts these choices. Its inputs prescribe the quotient and subobject of a short exact sequence, in that order. Its value on the middle term is obtained by integrating over its subobjects. In dimension three either bracketing integrates over the same flags of a line in a plane, which proves associativity of this product. With sheaves as coefficients one needs an actual map on the extension correspondence before integrating. If \(p\) records the ends and \(q\) the middle, the critical Hall multiplication calls for a morphism \(p^*(\mathscr P_\alpha\boxtimes\mathscr P_\beta)\to q^!\mathscr P_{\alpha+\beta}\). Comparing it with a local field operation requires transporting the square roots of the determinant lines and the moment parameters on the flags. The scalar product after integration cannot specify this map. [Extensions and their flags](#ch15-extensions-and-the-reason-for-associativity)

This distinction can be measured on a compact surface. For a K3 surface \(S\), the two ends of a relative Donaldson–Thomas tube carry states in \(H_d=H^*(S^{[d]},\mathbb Q)\), the cohomology of its Hilbert scheme of \(d\) points. Pair a state at each end and insert a divisor \(D\) in the interior. The reduced virtual correspondence defines an operator \(\widehat B^D_{\beta,d}\) in curve class \(\beta\). Closing the tube contracts its two ends against the Poincaré diagonal. The resulting reduced scalar series is \((\beta\cdot D)^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}\widehat B^D_{\beta,d}\) when \(\beta\cdot D\ne0\). Closure even changes the multiple-cover coefficient. After the specified cohomological identifications, each cover in the operator formula has coefficient one. The factor \(1/k\) for a cover of degree \(k\) appears upon dividing by the divisor intersection at closure.

What can one do with three open ends that this trace cannot do? At \(d=1\), choose a rational section \(C\) and a fibre \(F\) on an elliptic K3, so \(C^2=-2\), \(F^2=0\) and \(C\cdot F=1\). The first reduced multiplication in section class has an explicit expression on all twenty-four states. For the Poincaré pairing \(\eta\), the expression is
\[
\mu(a,b)=\eta(a,C)\eta(b,C)C .
\]
In particular \(\mu(F,F)=C\). Choose isotropic classes \(u,v\in H^2(S,\mathbb Q)\) perpendicular to \(C,F\) and satisfying \(\eta(u,v)=1\). Add the cyclic first-order term \(\nu(a,b)=\eta(a,u)\eta(b,u)u\). The fibre row is unchanged. For fixed \(a\), the change in the multiplication operator has trace \(\eta(a,u)\eta(u,u)=0\). Yet the three-boundary experiment with \(v,v,v\) changes from zero to one. The geometric three-boundary calculation selects \(\mu\). Every first multiplication trace together with the fibre row would still have allowed the rival. A comparison between Hall multiplication and compact states must recover this uncontracted operation and its orientation, even if it already reproduces the closed Igusa series. ## Preparing a state at the boundary {#opening-state-preparations}

The compact example keeps three states at their respective ends. In a field theory one must also be able to prepare the states used to make such a distinction. For the free current the two calculations can be done on different spaces. In the current Fock space the negative mode \(J_{-n}\), \(n>0\), multiplies by a variable \(p_n\). Its adjoint \(J_n=n\partial_{p_n}\) satisfies the contour commutator at level one. On the disk, let \(Z_n\) be independent centred complex Gaussian coordinates of variance \(n\). The substitution \(p_n\mapsto Z_n\) is an isometry on holomorphic polynomials. Indeed both norms of the occupation monomial \(\prod p_n^{m_n}\) are the square root of \(\prod n^{m_n}m_n!\). It extends to the two Hilbert completions. One can test the isometry on an observable sensitive to phase. The unit vectors \((p_1^2+p_2)/2\) and \((p_1^2-p_2)/2\) have the same separate occupation statistics. The operator \(2p_1^2\partial_{p_2}+p_2\partial_{p_1}^2\) interchanges \(p_1^2\) and \(p_2\) with coefficient two, so its expectations in those vectors are \(+2\) and \(-2\). On the disk the same two values are the expectations of \(2\operatorname{Re}(Z_1^2\overline{Z_2})\). It is a joint measurement of two harmonics. The isometry alone, however, supplies no field-theoretic preparation of a given state. To compare preparations one must find a state on each side by its own equations.

In the free abelian Chern–Simons half-space theory at level one, put two oriented charge lines of strengths \(a,-a\) above the points \(r,0\) of a marked unit disk, with \(a\) real and \(0<r<1\). Use constant framing, subtract self-contractions, and take the prescribed limit of outgoing separations in a fixed real direction. The current on an outer contour sees \(a/(z-r)-a/z\). The unweighted residue is zero. Weighting by \(z^n\), as in the first contour calculation, gives the eigenvalue \(ar^n\) of the positive current mode for \(n\ge1\). Independently, take a real harmonic field \(\phi=h+\bar h\) on the disk with constant mode zero, and write \(h(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}Z_nz^n/n\). Weight its Gaussian law by \(\exp(a\phi(r))\) and normalize. Completing the square gives \(\mathbb E Z_n=ar^n\) and \(\mathbb E|Z_n-ar^n|^2=n\). The squared modulus of the normalized ray wavefunction, multiplied by the vacuum Gaussian density, gives this same law on every finite set of harmonics and hence on the full sequence. [Neutral rays and a sourced disk](#ch11-neutral-ray-pair)

Here one can derive the equality of laws directly. The contour gives \(n\partial_{p_n}\Psi=ar^n\Psi\) for each \(n>0\). Their common solution is fixed up to its constant term and has the form
\[
\Psi\ \propto\ \exp\!\left(a\sum_{n\ge1}p_n r^n/n\right).
\]
On the disk, set \(h_N=\sum_{n=1}^N Z_n r^n/n\) and \(V_N=\sum_{n=1}^N r^{2n}/n\). The squared modulus of this exponential under the Fock isometry, divided by its vacuum expectation, gives the probability measure
\[
 e^{2a\operatorname{Re}h_N-a^2V_N}\,d\mu .
\]
Independently, the real harmonic source at \(r\) adds \(2a\operatorname{Re}h_N\) to the logarithm of the disk weight. Completing the square in each coordinate gives precisely the same measure. Since \(V_N\uparrow-\log(1-r^2)<\infty\), the holomorphic exponentials converge in the disk Hilbert norm and their normalized squared moduli converge in \(L^1(\mu)\). It is the whole density, including correlations between modes, which the two preparations agree on.

Keep the unit state fixed on one end of a cylinder and propagate the copy at the other end by \(q^{L_0}\), where \(L_0\) counts harmonic energy. On a ray represented by \(\Psi\), the reading is \(\langle\Psi,q^{L_0}\Psi\rangle/\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle\). The denominator fixes the norm *before* propagation.

Propagating through a cylinder of length \(L\ge0\) damps harmonic \(n\) by \(q^n\), \(q=e^{-L}\). The normalized overlap of the prepared state with its propagated state is \(\bigl((1-r^2)/(1-qr^2)\bigr)^{a^2}\) on both sides. The factor \(r^{-a^2}\) in the ray amplitude cancels from this measurement. An absolute cutting law must keep the vacuum amplitude as well.

For \(a\ne0\) these lines have zero total charge but prepare a state different from the vacuum. In fact the first two harmonic means determine where the marked line ended and what charge it carried. With \(\mu_1=ar\) and \(\mu_2=ar^2\), its position is \(r=\mu_2/\mu_1\) and its charge is \(a=\mu_1^2/\mu_2\). Varying the collar length separates frequencies because the successive powers \(q^n\) damp them at different rates. At \(q=1\) the normalized overlap is one, as it must be for a unit vector. Sending \(q\) down to zero leaves \((1-r^2)^{a^2}\), its squared vacuum overlap. Neither measurement can fix a common scalar multiplying an unnormalized filling. An absolute amplitude asks a question which the normalized state, even if known on every mode, cannot decide.

An overlap on the disk is positive when it is the squared norm of a state. Is every functional that can be written as a trace the trace of such a positive representation? In arithmetic one knows the number before choosing the space. Take a smooth test \(f\) supported in a small interval around \(\log 4\) on logarithmic scale. Of all the prime powers, only \(2^2\) contributes in that interval. Weil’s explicit formula adds the polar and real-place terms and gives a second expression for the same number \(W(f)\), namely \(\sum_\rho m_\rho\widehat f(w_\rho)\). Here \(\widehat f(w)=\int f(u)e^{iwu}\,du\), \(\rho\) runs over the nontrivial zeta zeros with multiplicities \(m_\rho\), and \(w_\rho=-i(\rho-1/2)\). Can translation on an actual space constructed from the integers give this trace for every test?

Scale the positive integer lattice by \(e^u\), evaluate an even Schwartz function \(v\) of zero integral with \(v(0)=0\) on it, and form \(\theta_v(u)=e^{u/2}\sum_{n\ge1}v(ne^u)\). Set these functions to zero as convolution relations, taking their closed span in the test algebra. Its tests and all their derivatives are integrable against \(e^{a|u|}\), \(a>1/2\). The Hilbert construction with weight \(1/2<s<\min(a,1)\) realizes convolution by each test as a trace-class operator on the quotient, with trace \(W(f)\). On the two-term complex resolving the quotient, subtracting a differential of a smoothing homotopy makes both component traces finite. Their difference gives the same \(W(f)\). The construction of this trace makes sense before one knows the positions of the zeros. [The quotient and its trace](#ch16-a-geometric-construction-of-the-full-trace)

To ask for positivity, require the operator for the reflected conjugate test to be the Hilbert adjoint. There is such a representation for the zeros for which \(w_\rho\) is real, equivalently \(\operatorname{Re}\rho=1/2\). It is a positive star representation of the same test algebra modulo its closed theta ideal. A zero of multiplicity \(m\) contributes on \(m\) separate layers. The difference of the two traces is exactly
\[
W(f)-\operatorname{Tr}\pi_{\mathrm{crit}}(f)
 =\sum_{w_\rho\notin\mathbb R}m_\rho\widehat f(w_\rho).
\]
If the two traces agreed on every compact smooth test, then \(W(g*g^*)\) would be the trace of a positive operator for each such \(g\), where \(*\) denotes convolution and \(g^*(u)=\overline{g(-u)}\). By the Weil positivity criterion this would imply the Riemann hypothesis. Conversely the remainder vanishes if every zero lies on the critical line. The quotient trace is a theorem without that assumption. It supplies neither positivity on every test nor a product for colliding arithmetic insertions. Here the distinction between a trace, a positive state and local operations can be posed without assuming any bulk dual. [The full trace and the positive representation](#ch16-a-positive-representation-and-its-exact-missing-remainder)

## A sphere insertion and an ordered collision {#opening-interacting-currents}

How does a bulk class give a number on the sphere? Choose a volume form. With volume \(dx\) at an isolated critical point, the sphere trace of a bulk insertion \(h\) is the residue of \(h(x)\,dx/W'(x)\). For \(W_t=x^3/3-tx\) and \(t\ne0\), the two simple critical points contribute opposite weights \(1/(2\sqrt t)\) and \(-1/(2\sqrt t)\) over a splitting field. Insert \(x\) and the two contributions are each \(1/2\). At \(t=0\) the same insertion gives the residue of \(x\,dx/x^2\), still one. The individual weights become singular at the collision, while this insertion and its sewing retain a value there. [The identity defect, residue and sewing](#ch19-diagonal-and-trace)

The scalar obstruction in the one-variable potential recorded repeated insertions of a single odd class. What happens to that comparison when there are several outgoing fields and their order matters?

A sphere residue gave a scalar. A contour around two gauge insertions can instead leave two outgoing fields, as the weighted contour did for \(W_3\). Work in the formal vacuum algebra of self-dual Yang–Mills theory coupled to an axion. Write \(J,L\) for its positive- and negative-helicity gauge currents. A transverse polynomial such as \(x^i y^j\) labels the Taylor component in two directions transverse to the insertion curve. Take the gauge algebra \(\mathfrak{sl}_3\) with the trace in its defining representation. Give the two positive gauge currents the matrix units \(E_{12},E_{23}\) and transverse polynomial labels \(x,y\), in that order. Their tree bracket contains the current with colour \(E_{13}\) and label \(xy\). Integrate the axion current with label \(x^2y\) counterclockwise around this current and divide by \(2\pi i\). In that residue, the coefficient of the product of the two negative currents with colours \(E_{12},E_{23}\) and labels \(x,1\) is \(+s^3/27\) at cubic order in the axion coupling \(s\). The lower-order brackets leave \(-s^3/27\) on the same product in the Jacobi identity. The cubic term cancels it. [The oriented residue and its Ward identity](#ch19-cubic-ward-identity)

Which part of such a collision is already determined by its transverse polynomial labels? First omit the gauge colour and field output and compute a scalar bracket on the transverse plane. On the plane with Poisson bracket \(\{x,y\}=1\), the two ordered pairs of cubic Hamiltonians \((x^3,y^3)\) and \((x^2y,xy^2)\) have Poisson brackets \(9x^2y^2\) and \(3x^2y^2\). The first scalar correction to their bracket takes the values \(3\) and \(-1\). The third Poisson transvectant differentiates each input three times and contracts the derivatives in pairs with three copies of the Poisson bivector. Dividing this contraction by twelve gives the correction. For a formal alternating bracket of these polynomials over a field of characteristic zero, take the ordinary Poisson bracket at parameter zero and fix its values on linear inputs at every order. Suppose each coefficient is a finite-order bidifferential operator with polynomial coefficients, covariant under translations and all linear symplectic transformations. Beyond the displayed first correction, Jacobi then determines the scalar bracket up to a change of parameter tangent to the identity. Comparing this scalar operation with the products of the celestial fields requires a map on those fields. [The scalar bracket on the transverse plane](#ch19-scalar-hamiltonian-bracket)

An ordered product of functions makes the remaining choice visible on \(x,y\) themselves. In the homogeneous parameter the third contraction in the bracket has coefficient \(t/12\). A compatible invariant product with a nonzero commutator normalization requires a square root of \(t/2\). Every nonzero square in \(k[[t]]\) has even order, while \(t/2\) has order one. Adjoin \(w\) with \(w^2=t/2\). The two products are now \(xy+w\) and \(xy-w\). Their commutator is \(2w\), giving the normalized bracket \(\{x,y\}=1\). [The coefficient ring of the ordered product](#ch19-scalar-associative-lift)

Restore the field outputs. Do their *ordered* pole coefficients satisfy the same three-insertion consistency test? In the formal vacuum of the pure \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) gauge theory with one axion, take \(K(a,b)=\operatorname{tr}_2(ab)\) and \([e,f]=h\). Place the positive currents \(J_e[x^2]\) at \(z\) and \(J_f[y^2]\) at zero. At order \(s^4\), extract the coefficient of \(z^{-2}\) and retain the terms with constant transverse labels in the output. With \(\nu=-3s^2/8\) and the ordered two-loop scalar \(-1/6\) on these two quadratics, the total coefficient is \(\nu^2(8L_h^2/9+44L_eL_f/9)\), where \(L_a=L_a[1]\) is the negative current of colour \(a\) with constant transverse label. The Jacobi test on \(J_h[x],J_e[x],J_f[y^2]\) fixes the \(L_eL_f\) coefficient that tests with a constant spectator leave undetermined. [The two-loop double pole](#ch19-marked-sl2-cubic-closure)

Increase the first transverse label from \(x^2\) to \(x^3\), keeping \(J_f[y^2]\) at zero. In the double pole, the coefficient of \(L_e[x]L_f[1]\) is \(28\nu^2/3\). The simple pole distinguishes which outgoing current is differentiated along the curve. The coefficients of \((TL_e[x])L_f[1]\) and \(L_e[x](TL_f[1])\) are \(26\nu^2/3\) and \(-2\nu^2/3\), respectively, with \(T\) denoting translation along the curve. [Quartic poles at transverse degree one](#ch19-quartic-labelled-poles)

How does Jacobi constrain the poles when both outgoing fields keep their transverse labels? Fix the cubic brackets of the gauge currents \(J,L\) and the axion currents \(E,F\), covariant under the linear transverse \(\mathrm{SL}_2\), together with the ordered three-field residues \(J(LL)\) and \(LLL\). At distinct transverse endpoints, affine covariance and a shear fixing the two points reduce the remaining Ward equation to a test on \(x^i,x^j y\) for every \(i,j\geq 0\). The open contribution cancels against the two orders of axion exchange in the double pole and in the simple pole with the derivative on the first output field. Polynomiality extends both identities to coincident endpoints. Thus the brackets satisfy Jacobi through \(s^4\) for all polynomial transverse jets and all their derivatives along the curve. [The Ward identity at every transverse degree](#ch19-quartic-current-outputs)

To put repeated collisions in a fixed order, one must know that every correction is lower than the word it replaces. Give a generator with transverse label of degree \(d\) filtration degree \(d+2\), unchanged by translation along the curve. The quartic terms with three and two fields lower this degree by two and four, respectively. Reordering therefore gives a free basis over \(\mathbb C[s]/(s^5)\) of ordered normal monomials in the four towers and their curve derivatives, with the constant mode \(E[1]\) omitted. Identifying these marked generators with microscopic renormalized currents and separating the individual graph amplitudes require further information. Higher orders in \(s\) and a physical coproduct for two lines remain open. [The marked cubic four-current algebra and its filtration](#ch19-marked-sl2-cubic-pbw)

## The amplitude before and after a cut {#opening-filling}

Multiplying a filling functional by a nonzero scalar leaves every normalized correlation unchanged. What would a second filling need in order to glue to it? If the amplitude takes values in a line, its value on the empty insertion is itself a section of that line. Where this section is nonzero, dividing every correlation by the empty value gives a normalized state. To undo this division when gluing two pieces one needs an identification of their amplitude lines, the pairing on the states at the common boundary, and the unnormalized value on the vacuum. A bulk observable \(O\) may be measured before the cut or passed to the boundary by its action. An absolute cutting law says that the two *independently* defined functionals have equal values on these corresponding insertions, including \(O=1\). This is the additional information lost by the normalized cylinder measurement.

On a solid torus with fixed metric and density, a quadratic gravitational model admits a normalized calculation after removal of the mode cutoff. Take a torus multiplier of modulus strictly between zero and one, nonzero coupling, positive \(\hbar\), and a sufficiently small smooth integrable source of zero normal trace admitting a smooth solution of the Ward equations. The cutoff even fields converge in mean square and almost surely in a space of distributions. Their limit has a Gaussian law, and graded Wick contraction gives the moments involving odd fields. In a reflected realization with a spectral gap, smooth source bounds and finite action integrals, heat regularization gives a nonzero local section of the vacuum line, the one-dimensional space in which the unnormalized vacuum amplitude takes its values. This determines how the normalization varies near a reference. The absolute geometric cutting law remains open already in this quadratic model. Each geometric half must prepare its own state, and pairing those states must reproduce the uncut amplitude, including its phase and its response to insertions. For interacting quantum gravity that comparison must also respect the interaction terms. A common phase of the entire amplitude cancels in every probability. A relative phase between components of a single normalized state can survive, even when the occupations of its modes have identical distributions in two preparations. [The gravitational cutting problem](#problem-gravitational-cap)

To formulate the missing cutting law for gravity, choose a classical solution in the Einstein–Cartan formulation. Its coframe identifies tangent vectors with the internal frame space and is required to be invertible. Work with formal perturbations about this solution, a fixed real form and a boundary polarization specifying which boundary data are held fixed. Fix the admitted regions, collars and cuts and a common ring for the quantum parameters. The full reconstruction conjecture in this perturbative sector asks first for independently renormalized bulk observables satisfying the quantum master and boundary Ward identities. Write \(\mathcal C_\partial\) for the geometric category of the admitted boundary conditions and their operators in the matched reduced current theory. Its geometric centre would represent coherent actions on this category, with their dependence on position, compatible with composition and collision. The local diagonal centre instead acts on the boundary field algebra \(\mathcal A_\partial\) at coincident points. For each admitted region \(U\), the conjecture asserts both equivalences in the diagram
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Obs}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(U)
&\xrightarrow{\beta_U} Z_{\mathrm{geom}}(\mathcal C_\partial)(U)\\
&\simeq\mathcal Z_{\mathrm{diag}}(\mathcal A_\partial)(U)
\end{aligned}
\]
where the first map is the coherent action of the independently defined bulk and the second identifies the two recipients. Both comparisons must preserve contacts, their homotopies and assembly of regions. Second, the admitted boundary objects should generate the prescribed physical sector, with its products of lines, vacuum and insertions. Third, there should be an intertwiner between independently defined bulk and boundary state spaces which carries the filling functionals and intertwines their sewing pairings. For an admitted filling let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{bulk}}\) and \(\lambda_\partial\) be those two functionals. In the identified amplitude line one should have
\[
\lambda_\partial(\beta_U O)=\lambda_{\mathrm{bulk}}(O)
\]
for every admitted insertion \(O\), and the same equality after sewing. At \(O=1\) it compares the absolute vacuum amplitudes. The conjecture is made in the fixed perturbative sector, with its specified cuts. Summing over topologies is a further physical question. [The state and the gravitational cap](#problem-gravitational-cap)

A coupling tells us which boundary operations an independently prepared probe can realise. The marked cubic and quartic show that their higher composition can carry an interaction that their binary algebra loses. Tensoring probes, moving them past one another and changing where they are joined test further parts of that description. For connected ordered coefficients the bar–cobar theorem recovers the operations and their mapping spaces. For the level-one disk one can also prepare the interior and compare every harmonic coordinate. For a gravitational filling the independently renormalised action, the boundary states and the amplitudes for cutting must still be identified in the same physical theory.
